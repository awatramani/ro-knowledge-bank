The Mindset

Demystifying the Field

Recently joined PA to RA:

"Wow, we spent last 3 days with the core team just to discuss the field planning even before we started the Field Staff Training. You, me and Santosh ji (Survey Coordinator, Odisha) have been spending hours discussing each and every small-big piece of survey and operational management in the coming days. Why do we spend so much time planning data collection? It feels like the most intense part."

RA replies back:

"That is exactly the point. Planning is one of the most demanding phases, and often the most under-documented. Rigorous planning sessions with the team provides practical roadmap to help us translate the research design into a workable field plan. It anchors the key discussions we need to have, what must be prepared, who needs to be involved, what 'good planning' looks like, and what support systems we can rely on when the field throws surprises at us."

The Core Philosophy

The Myth: "It's just a survey. We'll hire people, print forms, and go."

The Reality: Data collection may be seen as a logistics operation disguised as research. If you don't plan around people, process, payments and protocols, the science will fail. This section is your shield against chaos.

The Curveball & The Calm

The Curveball "You discover that the car vendor has declined to work just the night before the launch. The timeline is set, people are ready, but the cabs won't be there in the morning."

From chaos to controlled excellence: Fieldwork does not unfold on a tidy timeline. One week it is chaos to get everyone in the field, next week you come across a local festival closing half your sample. This is not a failure of planning; it is the context in which good planning proves its worth.

The Field Reality "A Surveyor approaches you looking flustered: 'Madam, the respondent started crying during the income module. I didn't know how to handle it, so I stopped.'"

The goal is not perfection: It is identifying the blockage and untying the knot. When a bus breaks down or a community leader pauses sampling, these are not disasters, they are routine roadblocks. Calm comes from robust planning, adequate training, sharing experiences, working out on issues and roadblocks and solving it, clear protocols, delegation and trust in the people to deliver.

Mastering the Field Realities

It is tempting to imagine a clean start at 8:00 AM and smooth handoffs. Experience tells a different story. Treat every disruption, whether a breakdown or a refusal, as a routine hiccup to be solved, not a disaster to be feared. Any field work that you are setting up from the scratch is a process and a journey to navigate the challenges and the way to address and mitigate them.

How to build resilience:

  • Map the Grind: Do not announce a start/end time until you know the local context. If the only bus leaves at 4:00 PM, your plan must adapt to that reality, not fight it.
    • Collaborative Solutioning: When deviations hit, the RA and the Survey Coordinator need to collaborate to identify and apply solutions.
  • Trust & Delegation: Avoid micromanagement. Give field staff the freedom to apply their wisdom. This does open room for errors and small mistakes but it is also an opportunity to train field staff, train your mid-level field team to learn on how to master these situations in the field. Trust is the buffer that absorbs surprises. At the end of the day, it needs to be a learning process for you and your team to master one additional skill and competency around field research.

Translation & Ethics

The Field Dilemma "The translation vendor says they need 5 more days. Your PA speaks the language fluently. Do you get your PA to translate it to save the deadline, or stick to the protocol?"

Before digitization, ensure your instrument is culturally accurate. A perfectly coded survey is useless if respondents do not understand the localized phrasing.

Do

  • Use certified linguists with NDAs.
  • Back-translate independently.

Don't

  • Use Google Translate.
  • Use internal staff for primary translations (risk of bias).

The 3-Phase Roadmap

The Late Night PI Request "Your PI emails late at night: 'Can we add a new module on Mental Health? We are already in the field.' Does this trigger a full IRB review or just an amendment?"

Phase 1: Post-IRB

Establish a "Change Record", a tracker to record all small-big changes made to the instrument with tight version control and record management with details of the re-submission and approvals for all the amendments. Needless to say, each and every change, even the minor ones need to be approved and validated by the IFMR IRB (In addition to other IRBs). Please consult JPAL SA's IRB coordinator (Director - Research) in case there's any confusion about anything related to IRB

Phase 2: Digitization

Lock the instrument. Ensure strict version control. Test logical flows and skip patterns on the actual tablet models.

Phase 3: Survey Logistics

Set up calls with the Survey Coordinator and Team Lead to map out the operational jigsaw puzzle before deployment.

Hiring: Grit over Resume

The Hiring Conflict "Candidate A has 5 years of experience but interrupts you during the mock interview. Candidate B is a fresher who listens intently but fumbles with the tablet. Who do you hire?"

Hire for attitude, train for skill. Fieldwork is all about resilience. An experienced surveyor with a rigid mindset will corrupt your data quality much faster than a willing fresher.

The Test: Don't just ask survey questions. Roleplay a "hostile respondent" scenario during screening. See who stays calm under pressure.

Piloting: Break Your Survey

The False Positive "You piloted in a quiet school that was conveniently located, you have had some communication with the school beforehand. You yourself visited and led the pilot in the school and were happy to see you managed to complete all 14 modules in the school on time. It went as you expected it to go on paper. 
Day 1 in the field: The field team reported to complete the school, most of the field teams were not able to complete what they committed. This is not the kind of people you would want to be a part of the survey; they are too slow. 

You failed to realize that they were not surveying a 'conveniently located school within 5 k.m. of their base location, the school they visited was 65 k.m. away from their base station. They had to take a but to travel 50 k.m. and then take a local jeep after waiting for 30-40 minutes. The jeep ride took another 1 hour to reach as it crawled slowly in the interior areas. The last 2 k.m. patch they had to walk and reach the school by 10:00 a.m. despite starting the journey from their base station at 7:30 a.m. after a long day yesterday. The school authority did not cooperate initially and some back and forth with the RAs and RMs finally helped to start the survey and by the time they competed 4 modules, the school time was over
Not taking the ground realities into account is the biggest mistake one can make. One can only start making these assumptions correctly after spending time in the field. That way the plans and decision making is more informed and not based on assumptions or 'How ideally this should have happened' "

A successful pilot is one that finds errors. If you come back saying "it works perfectly," you didn't test it hard enough. Test the commute, the environment, and the chaos, not just the tablet.

The "Stress Test" Rule

Don't pilot with friendly neighbors. Pilot with the busiest, most distracted respondent you can find in a chaotic setting. That is your real baseline.

Safety & Data Hygiene

The Judgment Call "It is 7 PM. A team is stuck with no transport. They want to walk 3km to the main road in the dark to save time. Do you approve?"

Data is never worth a safety risk. Field staff, at times, may take decisions that increases their risk exposure, just to hit targets. This is something that you need to fix, you need to setup expectations in a way that safety is never ever compromised. This can only happen once you start trusting people to take the right calls. You must explicitly empower them to stop work and abandon the target if they feel unsafe

Data Quality (HFCs) — Catch it early:

  • Duration: Too short = skipping; 
    Too long = respondent fatigue.
  • GPS Variance: are they at the right place?
  • "Don't Know" rates:  Is this an attempt to avoid typing?

Why Planning Matters

When a surveyor knocks on the correct door, turns on a fully charged tablet, and speaks to the respondent in their exact local dialect, it looks incredibly easy. But anyone who has actually run a project knows the truth. That single smooth interaction is built on weeks of relentless planning.

We are talking about massive data collection efforts. Imagine mobilizing 150 surveyors to reach 6,000 households across multiple states. With deployment just weeks away, the line between a successful launch and a complete operational breakdown is drawn right now. You cannot fix logistical failures once your teams are out in the villages. You have to anticipate and solve them during this exact preparation phase.

This guidance assumes you are organizing substantial in-person fieldwork.

Preparation goes far beyond filling out a calendar or checking boxes on a screen. It requires the rigorous work of translating academic theory into operational reality.

The single most important step is engaging the Research Operations vertical 12 to 16 weeks before launch. Compressing this timeline never saves time. It simply guarantees that mistakes happen in front of the community rather than in a controlled environment.

The Field Entropy Cascade: 

- Logistics delays from poor route planning reduce effective working hours. 
- Shortened hours cause missed targets. 
- Missed targets create pressure on surveyors.
- Pressure drives data shortcuts, fabrication risk, and attrition. 

This cascade is the single most common failure pattern in field research, and its root cause is always the same, Insufficient or unstructured planning.

Call 1 · T-12 to T-8 Weeks

Foundation & Design Lock

This is the most intellectually demanding call. Decisions made here constrain everything downstream, and errors here are the most expensive to correct. The RA shares the research design; the RO team (mainly Team Lead and Survey Coordinator) translates it into an operational feasibility assessment.

15 min Instrument & Design Status Review

RA presents the instrument status: which modules are locked, which are still under revision. Review the change record tracker. Discuss whether pending PI changes will require IRB amendments, and the extent of delay in plans and timelines.

Decision Required

Is the instrument content-frozen? If not, what is the hard deadline for content freeze?

Decision Required

Has translation done correctly? Is back-translation planned with PA or SC?

15 min Sampling & Site Information

RA shares the details around the sample sites, SC puts together basic physical details about the site intelligence and puts together a dossier that is shared before the call 2 is scheduled, the dossier should cover details around the settlement type (compact village, urban slum, scattered habitations), access conditions (road quality, seasonal restrictions), accommodation options for field staff (this is where RA and SC can discuss the right DA, any need for outstation DA), local festivals, migration patterns, any other existing issue, in addition to finding more about the local governance contacts (if possible).

Decision Required

How many geographies? What is the estimated team size per geography?

Decision Required

Are there accessibility risks that require field reconnaissance before planning?

10 min IRB & Permissions Strategy

Review IRB submission status and expected turnaround. Map and share timelines around approval requirements: district-level, block-level, and village-level authorities (especially where projects are partnering with Government departments). Assign who initiates outreach and when.

10 min Budget & Timeline Alignment

Confirm and share active budget codes to be used for field operations. Align on a realistic launch date by working backward from the Gantt. Discuss the "6-week red line", projects compressed below 6 weeks operate on a thin and narrow line with compromised planning and higher risks of issues.

Call 2 · T-8 to T-4 Weeks

Operational Architecture

The second call builds the operational engine. By now, Phase 1 gates should be cleared: instrument bench-tested, site information dossiers complete. This call focuses on people, logistics, and infrastructure, the areas where the SC's past experience and ground-truth knowledge is indispensable.

15 min People Management — Hiring & Team Structure

Review the staffing plan: total surveyors needed, supervisors, field monitors. Apply the buffer rule (invite 120% of needed staff). Discuss hiring channels, the SC shares tentative ideas around local channels and hiring strategies to bring in reliable candidates. Address the 90-day contract limit and 7-day cooling-off period.

Decision Required

Final team size with buffer. Has the hiring ticket been raised on the portal?

Decision Required

Screening criteria: dialect fluency, tech literacy, prior field experience thresholds?

10 min Training Design & Schedule

Review the 14-day training architecture (or whatever the planned duration for the training is) : Days 1–4 (org induction, ethics, study context), Days 5–8 (instrument walkthrough), Days 9–10 (mock interviews), Days 11–12 (field practice in non-sample villages), Day 13 (false launch & calibration), Day 14 (final assessment & selection). Confirm training venue, facilitator assignments, and material readiness.

10 min Vendor & Transport Management

Confirm transport strategy: vehicle type by terrain (sedan for urban, SUV for rural), vendor contracts with written agreements, driver protocols, fuel/toll/parking cost allocation. Identify 2-3 vendors and what are other requirements on procurement setup by A&F. Discuss field office setup, power requirements and conditions on the ground, charging infrastructure for tablets.

10 min Equipment & Office Management

Tablet provisioning status (request via RO portal, 10-14 day SLA). Training tablets. Field office setup: secure storage for consent forms, locked cabinet for PII, daily charging station R&R.

Call 3 · T-4 Weeks to Launch + 15 Days

Launch Systems & Quality Architecture

The third call activates quality systems, establishes daily rhythms, and sets up reporting architecture. It also covers adverse-situation protocols, wrapping-up procedures, and the critical first 15 days of data collection where the quality trajectory of the entire survey is determined.

10 min Daily & Weekly Reporting Architecture

Establish the reporting cadence: Daily Productivity Report (PA → RA, SC, RO Team Lead), Weekly Deviation Report (comparing field logs vs. SurveyCTO data, Sat–Fri cycle, due Monday EOD), and the Surveying Skills Assessment Tool (5-dimension scoring: protocol compliance, consent adherence, data completeness, duration compliance, probing quality).

10 min Core Team Calls & Feedback Sessions

Define feedback session attendance: Days 1–5 both SC and PA must be present. Days 6–12 PA continues daily, SC joins every 2–3 days. Continue until the team's Daily Quality Score reaches 90% for 3 consecutive days (RA approved). Evening feedback calls (30–45 min): productivity review (5 min), quality flags (10 min), instrument clarifications (10 min), team morale check (5 min), next-day planning (5 min).

10 min Quality Control & Adverse Situation Handling

Activate HFC scripts (high-frequency checks): flag short-duration interviews, GPS variance, high "don't know" rates, and skip-pattern anomalies.  Accompaniment schedule published and briefed. Adverse situations escalation to RA/SC; weather disruption, transport breakdown → Advance request to meet contingency expenses.

10 min Wrapping Up & Asset Return

Plan the wind-down: final data reconciliation, consent form collection and secure storage, tablet data backup and factory reset, asset return with verification against the register, driver settlement, vendor final payments. The PA submits the Field Completion Report to the RA/SC.

The Roadmap

Planning Timeline: What Needs to Happen

Planning is a cascading sequence of milestones. Field training cannot commence until the digital form is tested; vendor contracts cannot be setup until route maps are planned and finalized.

Phase 1: Foundation Setting (Weeks 1–4)

This is the most intellectually demanding phase. The RA finalises the survey instrument, coordinates with the data team in setting up SurveyCTO servers, including skip logic, validation constraints, calculated fields, and GPS capture. Bench testing begins by Week 3. The SC builds site information dossiers work sites. Permissions outreach efforts begin, starting in Week 1

The Late-Night PI RequestYour PI emails late at night: 'We need to add a new module on Mental Health.' You were planning to be in the field in a week's time, the latest changes are critical and would definitely need IRB approval. You will have to put your field plans on hold, potentially retain a few senior field staff at extra cost and let go other staff. Rehiring the staff again once approval is received will take time and resources, as field staff will be skeptical to join the project next time due to uncertainty of employment. 
While you will have to navigate these challenges and address them taking into account various factors. The version control tracker you put in place and maintain should capture all the details with respect to changes made to the instrument, the status and details around amendment approval secured, along with the field and financial implications. The information will help assess the reasons for increased per-survey cost at the end of the project.

Hard Gate: Details about data collection sites, route plans, terrain, travel arrangement (the cost to hire cabs, the time one can anticipate it will take to reach sites) and other logistical arrangements are complete for all sites and instrument is IRB approved, digitized and ready for field practice by end of Phase 1. No exceptions.

Phase 2: Operational Assembly (Weeks 5–8)

Before a single line of code is written, the English instrument must be finalized and locked. Translation demands strict independence: a certified external translator performs the forward translation; an experienced PA or SC who has never seen the original performs the backward translation. Once translations are verified, SurveyCTO programming builds operational guardrails — hard constraints to prevent impossible answers.

Simultaneously, the hiring engine activates. SC raises the hiring ticket with full details. Screening calls assess dialect fluency, tech comfort, and prior field experience. The buffer rule applies: invite 120% of needed staff to training.

The Translation Dilemma"The translation vendor needs 5 more days. Your PA speaks the language fluently. Do you let the PA translate to save the deadline? No. Never use internal staff for primary translations — it compromises scientific integrity."

Hard Gate: Team hired and training begun by mid-Phase 2.

Phase 3: Launch Preparation (Weeks 9–12)

Execute field reconnaissance over 3–5 days to verify actual road conditions, transit times, and local accommodation costs. During this visit, physically verify 30–50 households to test sample viability and migration rates. Finalize all vendor contracts — written agreements covering daily rates, fuel, tolls, driver meals, and contingency terms.

The 14-day intensive training begins. Tablet provisioning, field kits, consent forms, referral cards, ID cards, and authorization letters are printed and assembled. The logistics dry-run verifies every element under realistic conditions.

The Stress Test Rule"Do not pilot in convenient locations. Choose areas that represent average travel conditions. If your field team will rely on public buses, your pilot must use public buses. You must perfectly replicate the friction of actual fieldwork."

Hard Gate: Go-Live readiness checklist signed off. No partial launches.

Observe how ethical compliance requirements establish a rigid boundary before any actual training or field deployment can occur. Schedule the survey launch based on realistic compliance turnaround times rather than optimistic estimates.

You steer the research; we help you setup and manage survey and data operations.

The Research Operations (RO) vertical is not a "Help Desk" meant only for administrative emergencies; we are your strategic partners in field execution. Field Staffing & Recruitment

Hiring field staff remains one of the most critical elements in the overall data collection operation. Securing quality personnel is a significant challenge that depends on multiple vital factors. Securing an excellent team is never an accident. Even the best people require a conducive and supportive working condition to flourish and deliver high quality data.

Attracting and retaining top talent requires building an enabling environment through advance planning and transparent communication. The operational factors that create this environment include:

- Meticulous Operational Planning: Detailing every single element in advance, including precise travel arrangements, realistic route plans, and well-equipped base stations, ensures a smooth and positive experience for the team.
- Active Leadership Presence: Direct involvement in daily operations and consistent physical presence in the field builds team cohesiveness and operational trust.
- Transparent Contract Terms: Standard recruitment strictly adheres to a ninety-day fixed term limit, which must be followed by a mandatory seven day cooling off period.
- Clear Financial Expectations: The organization does not provide direct accommodation. Instead, a Daily Allowance is disbursed to cover local lodging and meals. Communicating this clearly before training begins prevents sudden attrition.

Pre-Field Ops › Mapping Households

Mapping vs. Census: What Is the Difference?

These two terms can sometimes be used interchangeably in conversation, but they are distinct activities with very different purposes. Understanding this distinction is fundamental before entering the field.

Physical Mapping

(Household Listing)
PurposeCreate a complete spatial register of every dwelling in the village so that no household is missed.
What you produceA numbered settlement map showing every dwelling, lane, and landmark, plus a total dwelling count.
Who & How LongA dedicated pair of surveyors physically traverse the settlement (0.5–2 days per site).
The AnalogyDrawing the blueprint of the village or a building before you survey every household in the vilage/apartment.

Census Enumeration

(Building the Sampling Frame)
PurposeCollect basic demographic and screening data from every listed household to build the sampling frame.
What you produceA dataset with one record per household: head of household name, size, and screening responses.
Who & How LongIndividual surveyors assigned to zones on the completed map (1–6 days depending on count).
The AnalogyInspecting every household in the village/apartment in the village/building using the blueprint as your guide.
The Key Insight & What Goes Wrong

Mapping ensures you do not miss anyone. Census ensures you know enough about everyone to select the right sample. Skip or rush either one, and the project is built on a flawed foundation.

Field experience reveals a consistent set of deviations. Surveyors, under time pressure or lack of experience, skip peripheral settlements, secluded hamlets, or construction-worker shanties behind a market. Multi-household structures or society apartments remain undercounted if surveyors don't enter and scan systematically.

The Operating Model

As a Research Associate/Survey Coordinator, you are the architect and accountable owner of the listing operation. You operate on three parallel fronts:

As Planner

You sequence villages into batches, size teams to match settlement complexity, secure advance approvals (where-ever possible), and build realistic timelines.

As Field Supervisor

You ensure the mapping method is followed precisely, that supervisors verify coverage in real time, and that exceptions are logged and escalated the same day.

As Quality Controller

You reconcile daily counts against map estimates, investigate discrepancies, and certify the final listing package before it is finalized for sampling.

Four Governing Principles

1. Exhaustiveness over speed

An extra half-day on a village is always cheaper than a revisit to fix gaps.

2. Method over memory

Surveyors who say "I know this area" are the ones most likely to miss a lane. Follow the method without exception.

3. Document over assume

Every locked household, boundary dispute, and ambiguous structure goes in the exception register.

4. Control daily, not weekly

The evening reconciliation is the single most important quality mechanism in the entire operation.

Pre-Field Intelligence & Planning

This phase happens at least a week before field deployment (ideally 2 weeks before the field deployment). The goal is to eliminate surprises. Every hour invested here saves three hours of crisis management in the field.

Why Pre-Field Maps Matter Any rule-based traversal method works well when dwellings are on roads or lanes. But in Indian villages, many households sit on open land, inside farms, behind revenue land, or along footpaths (pagdandis). These households are systematically missed by road-based traversal. The pre-field intelligence exercise identifies them before the team arrives.

For each village, the PA opens satellite imagery (Google Maps) and systematically examines five dimensions: 

- Household spread pattern
- Road and lane networks
- Peripheral habitations
- Off-road households
- Natural/administrative boundaries.

Permissions and Community Entry

Community buy-in helps in carrying out field activities smoothly. It is not a requirement and is also not guaranteed but in the early days, while you are still figuring our survey plan and during your exploratory visit to the field, try and get a sense of securing some kind of go ahead from community leaders (Sarpanch, Mukhiya, ward members) in the villages.  

Even a courtesy visit asking for verbal operations and then introducing PAs and Field Supervisor can go a long way.


Settlement-Specific Protocols

Rural Settings

The Sarpanch's verbal endorsement is top-tier. If not possible, engage ward members. Follow up verbal endorsements with a WhatsApp message for documentation. Visit peripheral hamlet leaders separately.

Urban Slums

Identify leaders through Anganwadi workers or Municipal offices. Address suspicion directly: explain this is a research project. Triangulate boundaries with 2-3 community members.

Apartment Complexes

Send a formal letter to the RWA President/Secretary 7–10 days prior. Follow up by phone after 3 days. Be prepared to present at the society meeting.

Sensitive Settings

Seek introduction through a trusted intermediary (health worker, teacher, religious leader). Deploy a smaller mapping team. Accept that multiple visits may be needed before mapping can begin.

The Traversal Method: Right-Hand Rule and Beyond

The right-hand rule is a deterministic traversal algorithm adapted from the "wall follower" method used in maze-solving. It converts a two-dimensional spatial problem (covering an entire settlement) into a one-dimensional sequential problem (following a single path).

SituationActionRationale
Street turns rightFollow the turn; continue numbering on the rightMaintains right-hand continuity
Street turns leftFollow the turn; right-hand side remains the referenceDirection changes but the reference wall does not
Side lane appears on the rightEnter the side lane; traverse it completely; return to main streetBranches must be fully explored before continuing
Dead endU-turn; number the other side (now on your right) back to junctionThe U-turn converts the left side into the right side
Emerge onto a previously traversed streetDo not re-enter; U-turn and complete the current branchPrevents duplication
Return to starting pointMapping is complete; verify all lanes coveredThe loop closes naturally
Critical Discipline Complete every branch of every street before moving to the next main street. If, while traversing a side lane, you emerge onto another main street, do not turn onto it. U-turn and complete your current branch.

Where the Right-Hand Rule Falls Short

The right-hand rule is a powerful starting point, but it has well-documented limitations in Indian field conditions:

Households not on any road: 
Dwellings on open farmland, hilltops, or across streams are invisible to road-based traversal (often 10-20% of households).
Ambiguous road definitions: 
Is a gap between two walls a lane? Different surveyors make different judgements, compounding errors.
Density bias & Interior loops: 
The rule spends more time in dense cores and may skip sparse peripheries or households trapped inside closed ring-road loops.

The 4-Step Strategy-Led Mapping Approach

1

Execute the right-hand rule along the road network.

The mapping pair traverses the settlement using the standard right-hand rule, numbering every dwelling on every road, lane, and pagdandi. This covers the connected portion of the settlement.

2

Conduct the peripheral sweep.

Using the PA's annotated map and the Supervisor's ground-truth sketch, the pair visits every peripheral habitation separated from the main cluster. Each habitation is treated as a mini-mapping unit with its own starting point.

3

Execute the off-road household sweep.

Accompanied by a local guide identified during village entry, the pair visits every household flagged as sitting on open land or disconnected from roads. These are numbered sequentially, recording GPS locations.

4

Cross-verify against satellite imagery.

After physical mapping is complete, the Supervisor compares the final map against the Google Maps imagery. Any dwelling visible on the satellite map that does not appear on the field map triggers a targeted revisit the next morning.

Pre-Survey Geographic Planning

Context & Purpose: Effective planning begins 2-4 weeks before deployment. Assigning teams without assessing the geography leads to blown budgets, wasted fuel, and missed daily targets.

Step-by-Step Geographic Mapping

  1. Acquire Boundaries: Map district, block, tehsil, and Gram Panchayat boundaries. Plot sample HHs on QGIS or Google Earth.
  2. Cluster the Sample: Group sample points based on realistic daily travel limits (e.g., maximum 40 km radius per team).
  3. Assess Terrain & Seasons: Identify paved state highways vs. village kaccha roads. During the monsoon or Rabi harvest season, travel times can double.
  4. Network Mapping: Identify mobile network, look into the plans to conducting surveys off-line, designated end-of-day sync points.

Daily Rhythm & Field Coordination

Context & Purpose: Without a strict daily rhythm, teams lose focus, data goes unsynced, and safety issues escalate unseen.

The Standard Operating Rhythm

  1. 7:00 AM - Briefing: Field Monitors/Field Supervisors define daily targets. Verify tablets/power banks are >80%. Ensure all enumerators have ID cards and other documentation.
  2. 1:00 PM - Mid-Day Sync: Field Supervisors collects progress updates. Identifies blockers and redistributes HH assignments via Phone call/WhatsApp.
  3. 6:00 PM - Debrief: Mandatory return to base. Connect tablets to WiFi hotspot, if data not uploaded yet. Sync all SurveyCTO/ODK forms. Complete the structured Daily Progress Report.
