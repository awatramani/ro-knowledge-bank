Training Academy › Overview & First Principles

Training is the most consequential investment into any survey. Almost all projects suffer if there are field staff who never truly understood the instrument, the protocol, or their own role. This section exists to educate you to make sure that never happens on your project.

It is written for the people who build and run trainings, Research Associates, Project Associates, and Survey Coordinators, not for the field staff who sit through them. Whatever your study (any of the eleven sectors, any geography, any respondent population, household or facility, in-person or remote), this is the place to learn how to design a training that fits it, deliver it well, and certify a team you can stand behind.

A Great Training, in One Line

Crystal clear R&Rs × Materials that teach intent, not just questions × Delivery that builds skill through practice × Honest assessment with acceptance that some will learn with more pratice = A Field-Ready Team

It is multiplicative, not additive. A surveyor who knows the questionnaire cold but has never been pushed by a difficult respondent will still freeze on Day 1. A perfectly facilitated training built on a program that does not include rigorous practice sessions and mock interviews and relies completely on the time invested 'walking-through' the sessions will still produce the wrong skills. Every factor has to be present.

1
What "Training" Actually Is

A System, Not an Event

The single most important shift in mindset: training is not an event that happens and ends. It is a lifecycle that begins six weeks before Day 1 (when the manual is first drafted) and continues deep into data collection (through daily feedback and recalibration). The well planned and rigorous projects treat it that way. Every compression of this lifecycle, transfers cost from the training phase, where it is cheap to fix, to the data collection phase, where it becomes catastrophically expensive.

A second shift: your job as a trainer is not to "cover the material." It is to produce a team that behaves correctly under field pressure when no one is watching. Coverage is an input; behaviour is the output. Everything in this section is organised around producing that output.

The line that makes it real "Mistakes in this room are free. Mistakes in the field cost the project." Say it on Day 1, and design the whole training so people make, and fix, their mistakes with you, not with a respondent.

Why It Is Worth Getting Right
Case: The 3-Day Training That Became a 3-Week Remediation (Bihar) A nutrition baseline compressed its training to 2 days; role-plays for household identification and mock interviews classroom practice sessions were skipped to "save time." The result: 34% of forms had errors; there were problems and issues in the quality of the work produced by several surveyors, the mistakes were repetitive; on top of it, 3 complaints from village leaders arose from skipped community-entry protocols. Total cost: three weeks of remediation and roughly ₹2.8 lakh extra. The training that was skipped would have cost about ₹40,000.

Training Academy › Diagnose Your Training

No two trainings should look the same. Before you design anything, you have to clearly understand the generic and study specific training needs of your project, a five-day phone survey on agricultural inputs and a fourteen-day in-person study with a domestic-violence module are not the same training at all.

This module is the diagnosis step that can help projects list down specific focus areas for their training program. One can work through the six questions below about their study;the answers determine almost every design decision that follows; duration/what special modules one must add/who to hire, and where the risks lie.

1
The Six Questions That Shape Your Training
Question 1

What sector & study is it?

The sector dictates the domain knowledge and specialist skills the surveyors need to know before they can even read the instrument intelligently.

  • Agriculture → Good to look at field staff who understand plot measurement, crop-cutting, crop rotation, unit conversion
  • Education → ASER-style assessments, classroom observation, child assent
  • Health → anthropometry, biomarkers, referral protocols
  • Finance/Labour → long recall modules, sensitive income/asset questions
Question 2

Who is the respondent?

The population determines consent procedure, communication style, and the special-population modules you must add (covered in Module 05).

  • Children → Assent + caregiver consent, age-appropriate technique
  • Elderly → pace, breaks, bystander management
  • Women-only / sensitive → female surveyors, privacy protocol
  • Vulnerable / tribal → voluntary participation, simpler language
Question 3

How sensitive is the content?

Sensitivity drives how much time you spend on trauma-informed technique, distress handling, referral, and surveyor self-care.

  • Routine (assets, attendance) → standard protocol
  • Moderately sensitive (income, debt) → privacy + empathy emphasis
  • Highly sensitive (DV, mental health, abuse) → full safeguarding module, mandatory
Question 4

What is the survey mode?

In-person and remote trainings differ fundamentally, in length, engagement tactics, and what you can observe.

  • In-person HH → community entry, navigation, field-pilot day
  • Remote/phone → calling protocols, identity verification, smaller groups, platform practice
  • Hybrid → Hiring protocols, extensive training 
Question 5

How complex is the instrument?

Length and complexity set your duration floor and how much walkthrough + mock time you need.

  • Short (~10–15 min) → 3–4 training days
  • Longer (~20–30 min) → 5–7 days
  • Multi-module panel / measurements → up to 14 days

Question 6

Who is your team?

Experience level and team size change everything from duration to whether you run a training-of-trainers.

  • Fresh hires → longer, more foundational, more practice
  • Experienced / refresher → compress theory, focus on what's new
  • Large team (25+) → consider ToT or phased parallel trainings

3
Generic vs. Study-Specific Knowledge

Every Training Teaches Two Layers

It helps to think of training as two stacked layers. The generic layer is the same on every study, how to be a surveyor: ethics and consent, org policies/ neutral interviewing and probing, Tablet/SurveyCTO navigation, tracking and status codes, professional conduct, data quality, and safety. The study-specific layer sits on top: your sector's domain knowledge, your instrument's modules and definitions, your population's special needs, and your study's protocols.

A common downer is to spend all the training time on the study-specific layer (because that's what feels urgent) and assume the generic layer "is obvious." It isn't. A surveyor who can recite your agriculture module but doesn't know how to take neutral consent or probe a vague answer will still produce bad data. Budget for both layers deliberately.

Generic: ethics & consent Generic: interviewing & probing Generic: CAPI navigation Generic: tracking & data quality Specific: sector domain Specific: your instrument Specific: population needs
🔗

The generic layer is largely pre-built for you

J-PAL SA's Onboarding Field Staff sessions and the Enumerator Field Manual Foundation Module cover most of the generic layer (org, values, ethics, roles, data quality, safeguarding). Schedule those into your training week and spend your scarce design energy on the study-specific layer.

Training Academy › Design the Program

With your diagnosis in hand, you now turn it into a concrete plan: how long, structured how, delivered by whom, to how many people, where, and at what cost. This is where good intentions become a schedule.

1
How Long Should Training Be?

Duration Is a Calculation

There is no fixed length or any standard duration for the training. Start from your instrument and adjust for the factors from your diagnosis. As a baseline from across J-PAL SA projects: a short instrument (~30–40 min) typically needs at least 7 days to develop strong surveying skills; a longer one (~60–75 min) anywhere from 10–15 days; and a complex multi-module or measurement-heavy study can run the full 21 days. These are of course some estimates based on past data and observation

FactorAdd time when…Save time when…
Instrument length & complexityMany modules, heavy skip logic, measurementsShort, single-topic, simple flow
Team experienceFresh hires new to surveying or CAPIExperienced team or a refresher round
Sensitivity & populationSensitive modules, children, vulnerable groupsRoutine topics, general adult population
Sector-specific skillsMeasurements, assessments, observationStandard interview-only modules
Always build in buffer Whatever you calculate, add buffer time. Trainings always run over: a module takes longer than planned, a pilot surfaces an instrument problem, half the team struggles with one concept. A training with no slack is a training that will start cutting corners on Day 6, usually on mock practice, the worst possible thing to cut.

A Proven 14-Day Backbone

This is the full-length structure used on large in-person studies. 3
Team Size & the Training-of-Trainers Model

Small Trainings Are Better Trainings

The more people in the room, the less individual attention each trainee gets and the harder it is to observe everyone during mocks. When sample size forces a large team, you have two good options rather than one giant session: run phased parallel trainings with smaller groups, or use a training-of-trainers (ToT) model (Preferred),  the RA, SC and PA trains a small group of staff (Field Monitor and Field Supervisor) intensively on all protocols and the instrument, and those trainers then run parallel sessions for smaller groups.

The ToT risk ToT only works if the trainers are genuinely trained. It works when the second tier of trainers is well trained and have been equipped to handle training effectively. Please identify staff who performing well and are showing visible sign of being great at training. Poorly-prepared trainer multiply their gaps across an entire group. If you use ToT, assess your trainers as rigorously as you'll assess surveyors, and as the RA or SC spot-check the parallel sessions.

Useful ratios to plan around: roughly 1 Field Supervisor per 4–6 surveyors, and 1 Field Monitor per 15–20 surveyors. Always recruit and train a 10–20% buffer above target headcount, some trainees will not pass certification, and you want to deselect without panicking about numbers.

4
In-Person vs. Remote Training
In-Person

The Default for Household Fieldwork

Richer rapport, easier observation, a real launch day (or a couple of days of false launch). Needs a venue with reliable power and space for breakout practice, printed materials, and transport logistics.

  • Book the venue early; check on power situation
  • Plan the non-sample pilot village in advance
  • Print manuals, character cards, quizzes
Remote / Phone

For CATI & Dispersed Teams

You are training people remotely to do work that is also remote, you can't see attention or body language, and connectivity will disrupt you. Design around that.

  • Pre-check every trainee's device, network, headphones
  • Pick a platform deliberately; teach it before Day 1
  • Smaller groups; cameras on; record every session
  • 90-min max blocks; attention checks every 15–20 min

Adapting the Mock Sequence for Remote

The mock progression (Module 04) still applies remotely, with platform-specific tactics: use breakout rooms for peer pairs, have trainees share their screen so everyone sees them navigate the form, and run supervised pilot calls where senior staff listen in on a conference bridge. The "senior staff as difficult respondent" stage works especially well on the phone, it mirrors exactly what a CATI surveyor will face.

5
Budget vs. Quality & the Contract Reality

Plan Around the 90-Day Contract

Every field staff member is on a fixed-term, non-extendable 90-day contract; a new contract can only be raised after a mandatory 7-day cooling-off period. If your survey runs beyond 90 days you will lose productivity to cooling-off, you may look into staggering contracts across the team rather than starting everyone on the same day, and factor this into when you train (and whether you need a mid-project refresher training for replacements).

Where not to economise When budget is tight, the temptation is to shorten training. Resist it.The cheapest line item to cut is also the one that creates the most expensive downstream failures. Cut venue frills, not practice days.
Training Academy › Build the Materials

A training is only as good as the materials behind it. This module covers everything you build before Day 1: the manual, the assessment, the quizzes, the mock materials, the demo resources, and the devices, and the standard each must meet.

1
The Training Manual

Training Manual is not a Printed Questionnaire, it's an Instructional Guide

The manual translates academic intent into field protocol. It teaches surveyors not just what to ask but why, and how to handle it when reality doesn't fit the form. It is jointly owned by the RA (scientific intent, ethics, survey logic) and the SC (local vernacular, contextual probing, logistics). Neither can write it alone.

Start at T-6 weeks, not the week before Drafting begins the moment the hiring request is raised. By the time you're interviewing candidates, the core modules should be drafted so your training architecture matches the skills you're hiring for. And before anything is printed, the final draft goes to the Director (Research) and Director (Research Operations) for sign-off.

The Question-by-Question Deep Dive

This is where most manuals are too thin. Break the questionnaire down not just by section but question by question. For every critical or complex item, the manual must define four things:

1The Intent

Why is the researcher asking this? ("We ask about fertiliser usage to calculate input costs, not to judge their farming.")

2Definitions

Localised definitions of complex terms. (What is a "pucca" house? A "joint family" in this district?)

3Tricky Scenarios

What to do if the respondent doesn't know, refuses, or contradicts an earlier answer.

4Observe vs. Ask

Which data points are asked, and which (roof material, sanitation) are captured by silent observation.

Worked example — Module 3, Q3.4 (agricultural labour) "In the last Kharif season, how much did you spend on hired labour?" — Intent: out-of-pocket spend on hired help; exclude family labour. Definition: Kharif = monsoon crop, ~June–Oct here. Probe: farmers forget cash paid at harvest, ask "Did you hire anyone for sowing? For cutting?" If paid in grain, estimate market value. Every red-flag question should have a box like this.
2
The Assessment & Daily Quizzes

Build the Test Before You Train

Design your assessment instruments during preparation, not the night before. You need two things: daily quizzes (10 questions — 5 protocol, 5 instrument, maybe use a Google Form, with answer keys) to surface misunderstandings each evening, and a final assessment across instrument, ethics, protocols with a scored mock interview. The PDRA framework in Module 06 gives the structure.

Frame quizzes as learning, never as exams Tell trainees plainly: "If you get questions wrong, it tells us we need to explain that section better." Otherwise people get anxious or copy, and you lose the diagnostic signal. Any question >40% of the room gets wrong is re-taught the next morning.
3
Mock Materials: Character Cards

Scripted Respondents Make Mocks Real

The quality of a mock session depends entirely on the "respondent." Write a set of character cards in advance, each a one-page persona with a backstory and the specific vague, evasive, or difficult answers to give, tied to the questions your instrument is most vulnerable on. Print at least the eight non-negotiable role-plays (Module 06). Prepare difficulty variants so a trainee re-tested the next day gets a fresh scenario, not the same one.

The complete household Migrant worker HH Hostile household head Over-helpful neighbour Reluctant respondent Sensitive-module privacy
4
Demo Resources & Reusable Aids

Build Once, Use Across the Training

Allocate preparation time to building resources that do the teaching for you and standardise the message: presentations with voiceover, PDFs with screenshots of each tablet step, and 3–5 demo audio/video recordings of model interviews. A demo recording is especially powerful, play it on Day 3 and have everyone fill the form independently, then compare against the "correct" version to catch systematic errors. Format everything to be readable on a phone, since that's what surveyors will reach for in the field.

Training Academy › Deliver & Facilitate

The quality of a training depends a lot on how it is delivered. This module is the craft of running the room, facilitation, the daily rhythm, the instrument walkthrough, the science of interviewing, and the mock-session engine that turns knowledge into reflex.

1
Facilitation Science

Three Principles That Separate Good Trainers From Great Ones

1. Never lecture more than 20 minutes without a practice exercise. Field-level adults learn by doing, not listening. Explain a section, then immediately pair people and have them practice it.

2. Normalise mistakes. "Training ke dauran Mistakes bilkul free hain, bejijhak apni baat rakh sakte hain, mistakes karo, mistakes hongi tabhi hum unhe sahi kar payenge, yahi woh jagah hain jahan humein sab kuch try out karna hai, mistakes ko bahar aane dena hai, training mein mistakes aati hai toh fayda hota hai use sudharne ka, main survey mein mistakes costly hoti hain ; mistakes in the field cost the project." Surveyors afraid of looking foolish hide their confusion and carry it into real interviews.

3. Use the "worst respondent" exercise liberally. Role-play hostile, confused, distracted, and non-responsive respondents. It is the closest thing to field reality in a classroom and it builds the confidence that separates competent from great.

Engagement tactics that work Call on people by name every 10–15 minutes; rotate who reads sections aloud; schedule hands-on practice for the post-lunch slump and lectures for the morning; keep blocks to ~90 minutes with breaks. One SC found engagement collapsed after 2 PM, especially after lunch, moving practice to the afternoon fixed it.
2
The Instrument Walkthrough

Teach the Architecture, Not Just the Buttons

The instrument walkthrough is the longest single block of the training (Days 5 –12 on the full schedule) and the easiest to do badly. The failure mode is reading questions aloud screen-by-screen while trainees passively watch. Instead, teach the questionnaire as an architecture: every module exists to answer a research question, every skip pattern and validation rule is there for a reason. When surveyors understand why the form is built the way it is, they stop being question-readers and start being data-collection professionals who can anticipate what comes next and flag genuine problems.

Go module by module, and for each, cover purpose, exact phrasing, response options, skip logic, the common problems, and the probing instruction. Pause every 20 minutes for a practice burst. For measurement modules (anthropometry, plot measurement, assessments), this is hands-on with the actual equipment, led by a trained trainers, not a slide (Anthropometry Trained Trainers: Hansa Parmar, Sabahat Ali, Santosh Kumar Sahoo).

"The device is your tool, but you are the instrument" No technology compensates for carelessness and no connectivity issue excuses lost data. Teach surveyors to own the data, not just operate the tablet.
3
The Science of Interviewing & Probing

The Skill That Most Quietly Decides Data Quality

Interviewing well is a teachable, neutral discipline, not a personality trait. The foundation is rapport and a genuinely neutral posture: no approval or disapproval, no leading, no non-verbal bias (nodding, frowning), no assumptions from a household's apparent wealth. On top of that sits probing: drawing out a complete, codeable answer without putting words in the respondent's mouth. If a respondent says "I earn enough," the surveyor cannot record "sufficient" — but they also cannot ask "so, about 10,000 rupees?" Both fail. The skill is the narrow path between them.

Technique

Laddering

Climb from a vague answer down to a concrete one.

"You said farming is 'okay.' Better, worse, or the same as last year? … About how many quintals?"
Technique

Strategic Silence

Wait three seconds after an incomplete answer; silence invites elaboration without leading.

"We had some money trouble." [wait] "…the hospital bill in March, mainly."
Technique

Echoing

Repeat the last few words as a gentle question.

"I work here and there." "Bas thoda idhar udhar karke gujara chal jaata hai? , "Here and there?", "Construction in the city, farming in the season."
Technique

Decomposition

Break an un-recallable total into parts and rebuild.

"Rather than the month, what did you spend yesterday? Was yesterday normal?"
Where to probe is the RA's call, not the surveyor's Technique is universal, but how hard to probe each question is a research decision. Over-probing a sensitive item is as damaging as under-probing a recall item. The RA specifies, per question, what a complete answer looks like, the approved probe, and the stop rule, written into the manual. Surveyors follow those instructions and, when unsure in the field, ask the SC/RA rather than improvising.
4
The Mock-Session Engine

Mocks are the bridge from classroom to field, serious assessment, not a warm-up. Knowing the instrument intellectually is completely different from executing it under the small frictions of a real conversation. Escalate through five stages; don't skip any.

1
Group, no forms

Walkthrough & Edge-Case Discussion

Read each question aloud; the group debates edge cases ("farms AND does daily-wage labour, how to code occupation?"). No tablet yet. Understand the logic before fighting the technology.

2
Individual, with forms

Silent Practice vs. a Demo Recording

Everyone fills the form independently while a demo plays. Compare submissions against the "correct" version, if everyone miscoded the same item, the problem is your training.

3
Peer pairs

Cooperative Role-Play

A interviews B (cooperative respondent) through the full instrument, then switch. SC & PA circulate, scoring against the observation sheet. Rotate pairs.

4
Senior staff as respondent

The "Worst Respondent" Exercise

Senior staff play difficult, vague, suspicious respondents while a trainee interviews and others watch. This is where probing and de-escalation are truly tested. This one can go off-track quite easily, pretending to be a worst responder can backfire, with responder trying to focus on animated toughness, therefore, one of the senior staff should show give some examples of being a 'worst responder'

5
Real respondents

Supervised Field Practice & False Launch

2–3 real interviews each in a non-sample village, observed, with written feedback and an evening debrief. The last chance to fix the instrument before it freezes.

When a Surveyor Doesn't Pass

1
Attempt 1 — structured debrief

Name the specific gap; demonstrate the correct technique. No sign-off.

2
Attempt 2 (next day) — fresh card

Re-test on a new persona; if failed, 30 min of one-on-one with the PA (and RA/SC, if present)

3
Attempt 3 — decision

Still failing a non-negotiable → don't deploy, or deploy only under Week-1 accompaniment, at RA discretion.

The hardest part of the jobDeselecting someone who invested a week is uncomfortable; deploying an unready surveyor is far costlier. Decide on evidence, not sympathy or staffing pressure. Always try and hire and keep some buffer to address minimum attrition.
Training Academy › Special Populations & Sensitive Topics

Some studies carry obligations and accountability far beyond the accuracy of the data. Preparing and training field staff on research ethics becomes even more if respondents are children, elderly, or vulnerable, and when there are modules that cover violence, mental health, or abuse, female health. The capacity development plans for field staff must focus on building skills that protect people, equips field staff to go over sensitive modules as expected. 

This is a non-negotiable and it is where compromised training program does real harm. 

Add the relevant parts of this module to your program based on your diagnosis (Module 01). If even one sub-section of your instrument is sensitive, you train the full protocol for it.

1
Population-Specific Add-Ons
🧒

Children

Assent from the child and consent from the caregiver; age-appropriate language; privacy without pressure; clear rules for when to pause or stop. For assessments: standardised, comfortable, one-on-one administration.

👴

Elderly

Slower pace; planned breaks; confirming understanding; and managing bystanders who try to answer for the respondent. Invite the respondent themselves to speak.

🤝

Vulnerable Groups

Plain-language; simple tactics when privacy is compromised; a clear stop rule when safety is at risk. Seek a better setting or postpone rather than push.

♂♀

Female & Sensitive Health

Female surveyors mandatory; privacy-seeking and dignity-preserving language; a defined protocol for when a family member tries to answer for her.

🧪

Health Measurements

Comfort and competence with measurements after hands-on training; cleaning and storage discipline; and recording the reason clearly when a measurement isn't possible, dignity is non-negotiable.

🌾

Farm & Livelihood Distress

Indebtedness, crop loss, and land disputes carry real emotion. Train trauma-informed communication.

2
The Distress Spectrum

Teach surveyors to calibrate their response to the level of distress they observe. Not every uncomfortable moment is a crisis , and not every crisis is loud.

Level 1Discomfort (normal)
Shifts in seat, pauses, nervous laugh, "this is a strange question."
Response: Acknowledge ("these are part of our standard form"), continue with empathy.
Level 2Emotional distress
Voice cracks, eyes water, long silences, references to hardship.
Response: Slow down; offer a pause ("Kya aap thoda rukna chahte hain?"); offer water; let them decide whether to continue.
Level 3Distress (Trauma level)
Trembling, starts crying, rapid breathing, going pale or very quiet, flat affect,
Response: PAUSE, put down the tablet, acknowledge it's hard, offer the choice to stop or skip, wait 30 seconds, resume slower or close the module supportively.
Level 4Risk / danger
Disclosure of ongoing violence, abuse, or suicidal thoughts.
Response: Follow the study's safeguarding protocol, listen without judgment, do not promise secrecy you can't keep, provide referral numbers, escalate, and report where mandatory.
3
Trauma-Informed Interviewing

The Protocol When You See a Trauma Response

For violence, abuse, and other deeply sensitive modules, train the response to trauma signs as a fixed sequence so surveyors don't have to improvise in a hard moment:

1
Pause

Stop asking questions. Put down the tablet. Gentle eye contact.

2
Acknowledge

"Main samajh sakta/sakti hoon ki ye baat karna mushkil hai." (I understand this is difficult.)

3
Offer choice

"Kya aap aage badhna chahte hain, ya hum ruk jayein? Aap chhod bhi sakte hain." (Continue, stop, or skip — your choice.)

4
Wait

Do not rush. Give 30 seconds. Silence is not the enemy.

5
Respond

Continue slower if they wish; otherwise close the module supportively and move to a non-sensitive section.

The Domestic Violence / Gender Based Violence privacy rule is absolute Domestic Violence questions must NEVER be asked when a husband, in-law, other household member is present. If privacy can't be achieved, SKIP the module entirely and record "not administered, privacy could not be ensured." This holds even when the Domestic Violence module is a small part of a study about something else.

5
Protecting the Surveyors Themselves
Vicarious Trauma · Self-Care

Self-Care Is Professional Maintenance, Not Indulgence

Surveyors who spend weeks listening to accounts of mental health, sexual abuse or suicidal thoughts, secondary  stress might be affecting them without them knowing it. Studies working with children on sensitive topics or projects working in the mental health space need to add-on additional layers of training and may attach some experts and counselors to counsel affected field staff. Please read through the Child Protection Policy or check with your Research Manager on support to be put in place for Field Staff on these projects around additional training and counselling. 

Train Surveyors to Recognise Their Own Signals

Projects working on sensitive mental health modules, working with people who are depressed need to perceive the higher risks associated with the project and should educate the field staff to read the warning signs and what to do about each: trouble sleeping or intrusive thoughts about respondents (talk to a colleague/supervisor, debrief); emotional numbness or "just fill the form" detachment; irritability or dreading fieldwork. Build in some light-touch debriefs after sensitive field days, access to the counselling service, and the clear message that asking for support is expected, not penalized.

2
Certifying Each Role

A field team is three roles, each certified differently. Train supervisors and monitors in a separate session before surveyor training so they can co-facilitate and assess.

Surveyor — the Core Track

Every figure in the dataset passed through a surveyor first. Certify on the full PDRA + a scored pilot. Train and assess: standardised interviewing, neutral probing, consent & ethics, tablet/SurveyCTO mastery, GPS & sync, and accurate recording under time pressure.

Instrument masteryNeutral probingConsent & ethicsCAPI navigationGPS & sync
The cost of a bad hireOne surveyor fabricating two interviews a day across 30 days produces 60 fraudulent records before reconciliation. Training is your cheapest defence; detection is the second line.

Field Supervisor — Quality at the Point of Collection

Manages 4–6 surveyors; first line of quality control. Completes the surveyor track, then a supervisor add-on: route & assignment planning, live accompaniments, enforcing replacement & callback protocols, approving start/end-of-day checklists, running debriefs, and being first contact for any issue or adverse situation. Certify with a simulated data review.

Route planningAccompanimentsReplacement protocolsDaily debriefsEscalation & safety
Train them to coach, not just catchA supervisor who only flags errors breeds fear and hidden mistakes. Train them to frame checks as standard procedure and learning.

Field Monitor — Independent Verification

Provides verification independent of the team producing data — that independence is the value. Trained specifically on detection: back-check calls and re-visits on 5–10% of completed surveys, spot-checks to confirm a household was visited, recognising fabrication signatures, and confidential escalation. Does not instruct surveyors directly.

Train the Distinction: Honest Error vs. Fabrication

The most important cultural message: an honest error carries no personal consequence; concealing one does. "If you made a mistake today, tell your supervisor today — it costs you nothing. The alternative costs you everything."

What happenedConsequence
Honest error (missed question, wrong classification)Reviewed; corrected where possible; no personal consequence
Concealing a non-contact (marking it refused)Private conversation; written warning; target review
Partial or full fabricationInvestigation; back-check of all forms; termination in most cases
4
Carrying Training Into the Field

The Daily Quality Tracker & the 90% Rule

Training continues into data collection. From Day 1, score each team daily on five dimensions (each /20, total /100; RA approves the rubric before launch). Intensive daily feedback continues until a team scores 90%+ for three consecutive days, confirmed by the RA — only then does the cadence ease.

Dimension (0–20)Scoring guide
Consent compliance20 = fully compliant; −4 per violation; below 12 = immediate retraining
Data completeness20 = ≥98%; 16 = 95–97%; 12 = 90–94%
Duration compliance20 = all in range; −2 per outlier; clustered short = fabrication risk
Probing qualityFrom back-checks & accompaniments; 12 = significant gaps
Tracking compliance20 = all protocols; wrong household = automatic 0
A persistent sub-90 score is a signalInvestigate: is one surveyor dragging the team? Is the instrument ambiguous? Treat it as diagnosis, not a verdict — and feed what you learn back into the next round's training.
