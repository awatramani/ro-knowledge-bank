1.1 · What This Session Is

A Field Operations Onboarding, Not an Org Tour

RAs and PAs already know J-PAL South Asia and the kind of research it runs. This onboarding skips the institutional tour and goes straight to how a round of data collection actually works on the ground, and where each role sits in it. By the end of the day, every participant should understand the full field staff journey, their own reporting rhythm, and the quality, ethics, and safety standards they operate within.

This first session sets that foundation: what each role delivers day to day, how often and to whom an RA or PA reports, who supervises whom, and the standards every field interaction is held to.

🎯

Why This Matters

An RA or PA is not just a data collector. Every field interaction carries the project's credibility. A fabricated survey, a lost consent form, or an unaddressed surveyor complaint is not an individual slip, it becomes a project incident. The standards in this session exist so those things do not happen on an RA's or PA's watch.

1.2 · Role & Reporting

What a Project Associate Owns, and the Reporting Rhythm

As a Project Associate, one is the engine of day-to-day field execution. The RA designs the project and carries overall accountability, but the field runs on what the PA does every single day. This section is about the PA's responsibilities and the cadence at which they are reported. (RA-specific responsibilities are covered separately in the joint RA sessions, so they are not detailed here.)

The PA Reporting Rhythm

Reporting is not paperwork for its own sake, it is how problems surface while they are still small. Know what is due daily, weekly, fortnightly, and monthly, and treat each deadline as fixed.

ℹ️ For PAs, The Field Backbone

An SC and RA who have a reliable, transparent PA can focus on quality and strategy. A PA who filters information, defers difficult tasks, or cuts corners on PII keeps the whole team firefighting. A PA's reliability and willingness to report bad news early are their most important professional assets.

Who Supervises the PA, and How the RA Fits In

A PA's day-to-day supervision sits with their Senior Coordinator (SC): the SC reviews the PA's tracker, quality checks, and escalations, and is the PA's first port of call. The RA is not hands-off, though, RAs play an active role in managing and supervising the PA's day-to-day activity and coordinate closely with the SC so that nothing falls between them. In practice this means a PA may hear from both; the PA should keep both informed rather than choosing one channel.

✅ Keep Both the SC and the RA in the Loop

When a PA updates the tracker or escalates an issue, the default is to inform the SC and copy the RA. This avoids the "I thought you told the other one" gap and means whoever is closest to the problem can act first. Coordination between the PA, the SC, and the RA is a shared responsibility, not something that only happens top-down.

🗣️ Situation: Catching a Problem Early

The situation: You are the PA coordinating data collection for a nutrition study running across a few sites. In Week 3 you notice that one supervisor is marking households “complete” faster than the team could realistically visit them. You hold off raising it: the SC seems busy, and you want to be sure first. By Week 4 a back-check shows that 38 of those households were never actually surveyed, and the consent forms carry a single copied signature template.

The project has to re-survey the 38 households, re-check every consent form in that block, and file an incident report. The review notes that the warning signs had been visible for over a week before anyone acted on them.

📌 Key learning: a PA’s job is to surface what they see early, the same day for anything that looks like fabrication or a data-protection problem. Flagging a suspicion that turns out to be nothing is always cheaper than catching it three weeks late.

🗣️ Situation: Treat the Review as a Setup Checklist

The situation: A project had been running for three months before a routine operations review was triggered. When the assessor asked where the hardcopy consent was kept, no one could locate 400 forms; they were “with the supervisor” and scattered across several team bags. A second check found that three supervisors had respondent phone numbers saved on personal phones “for coordination.” The project picked up two major findings and a 30-day remediation plan, which pushed completion back by six weeks.

The gap was simple: no one had a written, agreed plan for where consent forms lived or how supervisors were allowed to handle contact details. Everyone assumed someone else owned it.

📌 Key learning: use the review dimensions as a setup checklist, not a surprise test. Before data collection begins, and whenever you take on a tracker or a storage routine, ask: is there a documented plan for this, and do I know what it is?
1.5 · Field Code of Conduct

How Field Staff Conduct Represents the Organisation

When a field staff member enters a village or an urban neighbourhood, they carry the organisation's reputation, and the trust of future researchers who will work in those communities after them. The Field Code of Conduct sets the standards for conduct across three arenas: in the community, within the team, and with the organisation.

🚩 The Silence Problem

The single most dangerous dynamic in field teams is one where junior staff feel they cannot share bad news with senior staff. If surveyors are afraid to tell supervisors about non-contacts, if supervisors are afraid to tell PAs about data quality issues, and if PAs are afraid to tell RAs about problems, by the time the RA finds out, it will have been going on for weeks. Build a culture where reporting problems is rewarded, not punished.

Safety & Security in the Field

Core Policy · Safety & Security

Field Staff Safety Is Non-Negotiable

No survey, target, or deadline is worth a safety compromise. J-PAL South Asia requires all field staff to follow safety protocols, not because something always goes wrong, but because the consequences when something does go wrong without a protocol are severe and irreversible. These rules are operational requirements, not suggestions.

  • Never work alone in remote or unfamiliar areas. Field staff should always operate in pairs or teams, especially in new villages, urban informal settlements, or areas with reported safety concerns.
  • Set a daily check-in time. Every team must confirm safe return to base by a defined time (typically by 7:00 PM). If no check-in by cut-off time, the supervisor must immediately contact the PA, and the PA must contact the RA.
  • Do not work after dark. Field operations must conclude at least 30 minutes before sunset unless explicitly approved for specific evening surveys. Evening surveys require written protocol, a designated male and female escort, and a confirmed safe return system.
  • Know the emergency contact protocol. Every team member must have the PA's number, the supervisor's number, and the project emergency number saved. The project must maintain a "field emergency card" with local police, hospital, and J-PAL contact details.
  • Assess and adapt. If a community feels unsafe on a given day, local conflict, hostile group, hostile community leader, teams have the authority to withdraw and report to the PA/RA without completing their target. No surveyor should be pressured to enter an unsafe situation to meet numbers.

Context-Specific Safety Protocols

ContextKey Safety ConsiderationsProtocol
Rural HH SurveyRemote locations, limited connectivity, travel on unlit roads, unfamiliar villagesTeam of minimum 2; check-in at noon and 7 PM; share daily route with PA; inform local contact of arrival and departure
Urban SAS SurveyDense informal settlements, potential for theft, male surveyors in sensitive areas with women respondentsFemale surveyors in pairs for areas with high safety risk; project phone (not personal phone) for respondent contact; no late evening visits
Remote / CATI SurveyWorking from home/office but isolation risks, long hours, ergonomic issuesRegular check-ins with supervisor; no sharing respondent data on personal devices; online safety protocols for video calls
🗣️ Situation: Why the Check-in Protocol Exists

The situation: A two-person team finished their assignments and were heading back to base in the evening. They missed the agreed check-in time, and the supervisor could not reach them because they were in a low-network area. As the PA, you escalate to the RA within fifteen minutes. With local contacts alerted and the supervisor sent along the last known route, the team is found safe within the hour: a flat tyre and a dead phone battery, no one hurt.

What mattered is that the missed check-in is the reason anyone went looking. Without that one routine protocol, the team could have waited on an unlit road for hours with no one knowing.

📌 Key learning: safety protocols are built for the one time they are needed. The ninety-nine times nothing goes wrong, the check-in feels routine. The one time it does, it is the difference between a minor delay and a real emergency.
  • Project emergency card printed and distributed to all team members
  • Daily check-in time set and communicated to all supervisors and PAs
  • Team pairing rule communicated, no solo field visits in new areas
  • Evening cut-off time established (default: 30 min before sunset)
  • Local emergency contact numbers (police, hospital) saved in project phone
  • Escalation protocol: Supervisor → PA → RA → RM within defined timeframes

Two Truths and a Research Lie, with a Role Twist

This exercise does two things at once: breaks the ice and anchors the session's core concepts in participants' own experiences.

Instructions:

  1. Each participant prepares 3 statements about themselves: two true personal facts, and one statement about their role (RA or PA) that they believe to be true, which may or may not be accurate based on today's session.
  2. The group guesses which personal statement is false, and discusses whether the role statement is accurate.
  3. Facilitator uses the role statement discussion to reinforce key learnings from Section 1.2.

Example role statements participants might make (some accurate, some not):

  • "As a PA, it's not my job to understand PII, that's handled higher up." (False, PAs implement PII protocols and keep the consent register daily)
  • "As a PA, if I report to my SC I don't also need to keep the RA informed." (False, keep both the SC and RA in the loop; copy the RA)
  • "As a PA, I should escalate any issue that hasn't resolved within 24 hours." (True, and safety issues immediately)

Debrief Question for Facilitator: "Which reporting or escalation step did the group think was optional that is actually expected?"

🎮 Exercise
⏱ 20 minutes

Map the Reporting Week

Working in small groups, each PA lays out a typical field week and slots every reporting and review task into the right cadence: daily, weekly, fortnightly, or monthly. For each item, note who it goes to, SC, RA, or both.

After 10 minutes, compare across groups. The facilitator clarifies any item placed in the wrong cadence or sent to the wrong person, these mismatches are exactly where things slip in real fieldwork.

Commonly misplaced items: same-day safety/PII flags (often wrongly held for the weekly report), quality-check status (weekly, not monthly), and asset/consent reconciliation (fortnightly).

A PA's SC supervises their day-to-day work, but the RA also messages them directly asking for field updates. The best way for the PA to handle reporting is to:

A. Acceptable if the apartment has a locked cupboard
B. Acceptable as a temporary measure for maximum 1 week
C. An IRB violation regardless of the lock situation
D. Acceptable if the RA has approved it in writing
✅ C is correct. Home storage fails every IRB test, there is no access control, no institutional authority, no risk review trail. 2.1 · The Big Picture

From a Name on a List to a Contracted Surveyor

Field staff don't simply appear. Each one moves through a defined pipeline before they ever collect a single survey. An RA or PA touches several of these steps directly, and needs to understand the ones they don't, so they can answer candidates' questions, set expectations, and spot when something is stuck.

ℹ️ Why Knowing All of It Matters

Even the steps an RA or PA doesn't personally run will reach their desk as questions, "When do I get paid?", "Why hasn't my contract come through?", "What documents do I still owe?" Knowing the pipeline lets them give a straight answer and chase the right person instead of guessing.

⚠️ Screen for the Job, Not Just the CV

Over-weighting prior experience can screen out trainable, locally rooted candidates and screen in people who survey on autopilot. The parameters are a filter, not a ranking, the real test of readiness comes at the post-training assessment.

2.3 · Invitation & Offer Letter

Inviting Candidates and Issuing the Trainee Offer Letter

Once a candidate clears screening, they are formally invited to training and issued a Trainee Offer Letter. This is the document that sets the terms of the training relationship, and it is important that candidates understand it is an offer of training, not yet an offer of employment.

📎 Reference Document

Use the standard template, Trainee Offer Letter (TOL) template, and fill in the project code, designation, dates, and reward amount. (In edit mode this link can be repointed to wherever the live template is hosted.)

What the Offer Letter Says

  • It offers a training contract as a stated designation, for a fixed start and end date, on the project's terms.
  • The trainee receives a lumpsum reward per day from project funds, payable on satisfactory completion of the training.
  • Training-related travel is reimbursed per the rules; a one-time travel reimbursement up to a stated cap may apply, depending on funding.
  • Being absent for 2 consecutive days without notice is treated as opting out of the training.
  • It is a full-time schedule; either side can end it on 2 days' notice.
  • It is explicitly a training opportunity only, not a fixed-term work contract. Any further engagement depends on performance during training.
  • Annexures the candidate signs: Data Confidentiality Agreement, J-PAL Code of Conduct, Harassment-Free Workplace Policy, and a Self-Declaration (confirming they are 18+, and have no pending child-protection or criminal matters).

⚠️ Set the Expectation Honestly

Candidates sometimes hear "offer letter" and assume they have been hired. Be clear: this letter covers the training period only. The main contract comes later, and only after training performance and document verification. Saying this up front prevents a difficult conversation at the assessment stage.

2.6 · Post-Training Assessment

Assessing Candidates, The RA's Critical Judgement Call

This is the step where the RA takes a critical, hands-on role. After training, the RA assesses who is ready to go to the field. The decisions made here shape the quality of the entire round, so they deserve real thought, not a rushed pass/fail.

⚖️ Be Rigorous, but Leave Room for Growth

Assess honestly, someone who can't follow the instrument or handle consent correctly should not be sent to households. But being too stern or rigid is impractical: very few candidates are polished on day one, and a strict cut-off can leave a project without enough field staff and discard people who would become strong surveyors with a little more practice. Where a candidate is close, it is often right to give them the opportunity to improve with practice, a short supervised period, a re-check, or a slower start, rather than dropping them outright.

2.7 · Verification & Contract

Document Verification and the Main Contract

Once a candidate passes the assessment, the final step happens largely in the background. The DM Data Management team takes over the documents and clears the way for the main contract.

What the DM Team Does

  • Verifies the documents submitted at collection, checking they are complete, legible, and consistent with the candidate's details.
  • Verifies the Aadhaar to PAN linkage, confirming the two are linked before payment and contracting can proceed.
  • Updates the records in the system so that the main contract can be processed.

✅ The RA/PA Job: Hand DM a Clean Set

DM verification is fast when the inputs are clean and slow when they are not. The most common hold-ups are an unlinked PAN, a name mismatch between the bank passbook and ID, or a missing document. Catching these at collection saves the candidate, and the RA or PA, a delayed contract.

ℹ️ Training Pass ≠ Contract

Passing training and clearing verification are two separate gates. A candidate can be excellent in training and still be held up by a document issue. Set the expectation that the main contract follows verification, not the assessment result alone.

2.1 · Team Composition

Building the Right Team for the Survey

The RA's first operational decision is team architecture: how many people, in what roles, covering what geography. Get this wrong and the project will either overspend or under-deliver. The right team structure flows from the sample design, not from what was convenient on a previous project.

✅ The Team Sizing Formula

Step 1: Understand the daily target (review the assumptions sheet in the project budget), how many completed surveys per day across the project?
Step 2: Estimate per-surveyor daily productivity: 3 to 5 surveys/day (HH, rural), 5 to 7/day (urban slums), 10 to 15/day (or more for CATI).
Step 3: Divide target by per-surveyor productivity. Add some buffer (maybe 15%, please check for past experience from other projects) buffer for refusals, travel, re-visits.
Step 4: Organise surveyors into teams of 4 to 6 per supervisor.
Step 5: Add 1 monitor per 2 to 3 teams.

Example: Target = 40 surveys/day. At 4/surveyor/day → 10 surveyors + 15% = 12. 2 teams of 6, each with 1 supervisor. 1 monitor. Total: 12 + 2 + 1 = 15 field staff.

🗣️ Situation: Do Not Copy a Team Structure

The situation: As the RA, you set up a team for a rural health survey using one supervisor for every ten surveyors, borrowing the structure from an earlier, more concentrated urban project. In the field the supervisors cannot accompany surveyors daily, cannot collect consent forms each evening from all ten, and miss quality issues for days at a time. By Week 3 the back-check failure rate is 18 percent, when anything above 5 percent is a concern.

The problem was not the surveyors. A ratio of one to ten only works where a team is geographically tight. Rural work spreads supervisors across villages and needs a lower ratio.

📌 Key learning: team ratios are context-dependent. Never copy a structure from a different setting. Build it from the geography and productivity realities of the specific project in front of you.

The Two-File PII System (Critical)

Any risk review assessment on the project will specifically asks: "Do the trackers contain respondent PII such as Name, Phone, Address, or ID details?" Most teams inadvertently embed PII in operational trackers. The solution is the Two-File System.

✅ File A: Operational Tracker (Shared)

Contains: Respondent codes, village codes, survey status, surveyor code, date, flags. No names, no phones, no addresses. Visible to all field staff. Shareable in team WhatsApp for daily updates.

ℹ️ File B: PII Linkage File (Restricted)

Maps codes to names, phones, and addresses. Access restricted to RA, SC & PA only, view-only, no download. Stored in a protected Google Sheet. PAs access only with RA authorization for specific tasks.

🚩 The Silent PII Violation

Many RAs don't realize their tracking sheets contain PII. If a performance tracker includes respondent names or phone numbers alongside survey IDs, that is a PII document and a violation. If a team's WhatsApp group is being used to report H/H or respondent details, it's a PII violation and must be treated as one. Check every tracker before deployment: if a specific household member can be identified from it, it contains PII.

🗣️ Situation: Sensitive Data in the Wrong Place

The situation: As the PA, you are coordinating respondent visits over the team chat group. To save time, you post a screenshot of the tracking sheet that happens to include respondent names, phone numbers, and health status alongside their locations. A surveyor’s phone is later stolen. That sensitive respondent data is now sitting on an unsecured, stolen device, and the project has to notify the ethics board, contact everyone affected, and put emergency data-protection steps in place.

📌 Key learning: a chat group is a coordination tool, not a data platform. Never share tracking sheets, respondent lists, or any personal data through it. Design trackers so the version that goes into the chat carries zero personal data, only codes and status flags.

3.1 · Research Ethics

Why Ethics Is a Field Issue, Not a Classroom Concept

Research ethics is not a box to tick during training, it is the operating system of everything done in the field. Every decision a surveyor or supervisor makes about how to approach a respondent, how to administer consent, how to handle a disclosure of harm, and how to protect the collected data is an ethical decision. This session makes those abstract principles concrete and field-specific.

The Four Core Principles, Applied to Field Work

PrincipleWhat It MeansHow It Plays Out in the Field
Respect for PersonsRespondents are autonomous individuals with the right to decide whether to participateInformed consent is not a formality. The respondent must genuinely understand what they are agreeing to. Rushing consent violates this principle, even if the form is signed.
BeneficenceThe research should benefit participants and society; at minimum, it should not harm themIf a respondent discloses domestic violence, mental illness, or child abuse during a survey, the surveyor has a duty of care that extends beyond the questionnaire. Field staff should know the referral protocols.
Non-MaleficenceDo not cause harm through the research process itselfAsking sensitive questions about income, violence, or sexual health can be harmful in certain contexts, especially in the presence of a spouse or authority figure. Ensure privacy before sensitive modules.
JusticeResearch benefits and burdens should be fairly distributedDo not systematically exclude certain households (e.g., distant ones, difficult interviewers) to make the work easier. The sample is designed to be representative, substituting undermines justice.

🔬 IRB Approval Is Not a Blanket Clearance

The IRB approves the research protocol, the way the study was described. Every deviation from that protocol (using a modified consent form, skipping a module for "difficult" respondents, changing the respondent eligibility criteria in the field) is a protocol violation that may require IRB notification. When in doubt about a field adaptation, field staff should consult the RA/RM before implementing it.

3.2 · Informed Consent in Practice

Consent Is a Process, Not a Signature

The most important sentence in research ethics training: consent is a process, not a signature on a form. The respondent must genuinely understand, in their own language, at their own pace, who the surveyor is, what the research is about, what will be asked, how long it will take, that their answers are confidential, and that they can stop at any time. A surveyor who rushes through the consent script in 30 seconds and hands a pen to the respondent has violated the consent process, even if the form is signed.

The Six Elements of Valid Informed Consent

🚩 Consent Invalidators, Know These

  • Coercion: "The sarpanch said everyone must participate", this is coercion. Consent must be voluntary.
  • Deception: "We're from the government" or "You'll get a certificate/benefit" (unless true and approved by IRB)
  • Rushing: Reading the consent script while the respondent is clearly distracted or has not been given time to process
  • Child signing: A child under 18 cannot provide consent. A parent or guardian must sign for them.
  • Proxy consent: "Her husband said it's fine", the husband's permission is not the woman's consent. Each eligible respondent must consent for themselves.
  • Incentive pressure: "If you complete this survey you get ₹50", incentives are permitted if IRB-approved, but they must not be the reason someone feels they cannot refuse.
🗣️ Situation: A Thumbprint Is Not Consent

The situation: Running well behind schedule, a surveyor reaches a household where an older woman who cannot read answers the door. He hands her the consent form and says, “Just sign here, it is for a health study.” She makes her mark without being told anything about the study, and the interview is finished in 22 minutes against a designed 35.

In a routine back-check, she is asked what the study was about. She says only that “a young man came and asked some questions.” She had no memory of being told about confidentiality or her right to stop. The consent is judged invalid, the interview has to be redone properly, and the surveyor is flagged for refresher training.

📌 Key learning: a thumbprint without understanding is not consent. When a respondent cannot read, the surveyor must read the whole consent form aloud, in the respondent’s language, and confirm they understood before starting.

Core Policy · Mandatory · Now a 2-Hour Session

POSH, Prevention of Sexual Harassment

Field deployments concentrate the exact conditions where POSH risks are elevated: power differentials between supervisors and surveyors, physical isolation from institutional support, long working hours in unfamiliar settings, shared accommodation, and a culture of normalising difficult conditions as “that’s just how fieldwork is.” This deep-dive equips RAs and PAs to run the POSH conversation with their teams, to recognise harm early, protect the person affected, and make speaking up feel safe. The session is built around the same principle the policy rests on: this is about protection, not rules.

The One Test That Matters, “Unwelcome”

The test is not whether something was meant as harassment. It is whether it was experienced as unwelcome by the person who received it. “I was only joking” is not a defence, not under the POSH Act, 2013, and not at J-PAL SA. Intention is not the measure; impact on the listener is.

Who & Where It Covers

  • Who: every field staff member, regardless of role, gender, or seniority. Surveyors, Supervisors, Field Monitors, drivers, and community members encountered in the field are all within scope.
  • Where: not just the office. The field site, shared accommodation, travel, team WhatsApp groups, and “private” messages are all the workplace. Wherever you are because of J-PAL work, this policy protects you.

What Constitutes Sexual Harassment, With Examples

FormWhat it can look like at workCovered?

Verbal / sexually-coloured remarksComments on appearance framed as “compliments,” persistent questions about someone’s relationships or personal life, or jokes with a sexual undertone, including in the team chat.Yes, the test is whether it was unwelcome, not whether it was “meant kindly.”

Even on a Lighter Note, Words Carry Weight

Humour is part of a good team; we are not asking anyone to stop laughing. We are asking one thing: be mindful of your words. A comment can be “just a joke” to the person saying it and genuinely uncomfortable to the person hearing it, or to someone quietly listening nearby. The line is the listener, not the intention.

  • A comment meant kindly: “So when are you finally settling down? You are not getting any younger,” said warmly over coffee, can land as pressure or intrusion on someone who would never say so out loud.
  • A joke about someone’s looks: “Someone made an effort today, who are we trying to impress?” is light-hearted to the speaker, and singling-out and personal to the person it is aimed at.
  • Teasing about who sits with whom: playfully pairing two colleagues romantically can embarrass both and follow them through the whole project.
  • Talking about a third person who is not there: remarks on a colleague’s body or dress, speculation about someone’s personal life, or imitating someone for a laugh all fall under the same line. If you would not say it with that person standing next to you, do not say it when they are not.

  • 🔒 Zero retaliation. No one who raises a concern, or who is a witness, may be penalised for it. Retaliation (worse assignments, exclusion, an unfair review, cold treatment) is itself a POSH violation, treated just as seriously as the original conduct.
  • 🤝 Confidentiality. What you share is kept to the people who must know to act on it. Whoever first receives a concern does not investigate it themselves and does not tell the rest of the team, they pass it to the right place and keep it private.
📌 For RAs & PAs who coordinate a team: leadership sets the temperature. Keep your own remarks clean, in person and in the group chat, and address the first uncomfortable joke, not the tenth. Offer comfort, not pressure. Never dismiss, trivialise, or “resolve informally.”

Knowing What Is OK, What Is Not, and Feeling Able to Say So

Plenty of people, especially women early in their careers, carry a quiet question: “Am I even allowed to feel uncomfortable about this?” The answer is yes. Your experience is the test. You do not have to be certain, you do not have to confront anyone, and you do not have to wait for something worse to happen. Use this as a plain guide.

✅

Normal, welcome team behaviour

  • Friendly chat and inside jokes the whole team enjoys, with no one as the target.
  • A teammate checking in about a deadline or your workload through the usual work channels.
  • Specific, work-related feedback on your analysis or your memo, not on you as a person.
  • Being asked once whether you are comfortable taking something on, and your answer respected.
🚩

Not OK, even if it seems small

  • Personal messages or late-night DMs that drift away from work, especially from someone senior.
  • Comments on your appearance or your personal life, even when framed as a compliment.
  • Being the standing punchline of a joke in the team channel while everyone laughs along.
  • Any hint that being “easy to get along with” or always available will shape your review, your references, or the projects you get.
  • Attention that keeps finding its way back to your personal time, your inbox, or your social accounts.
🧭

Five signals that something has crossed the line

If you notice any of these in yourself, take it seriously. It is information, not an over-reaction.

  • You have started changing your own behaviour to avoid someone: muting a channel, skipping an optional call, rearranging your day.
  • You feel you have to laugh along at something you do not find funny.
  • A “friendly” message makes your stomach drop when you see who sent it.
  • You are rehearsing how to explain it, and bracing to be told you are overreacting.
  • You would be uneasy if the same thing were said or sent to a close friend.

A polite smile is not consent. It is often how someone copes when speaking up feels impossible. Your discomfort is valid even when no one intended any harm.

🗣️

Words you can borrow, for the person or for someone you trust

  • If you feel comfortable saying it directly (you never have to): “Could we keep our messages to work? Thanks.”
  • To someone you trust, your RA, your SC, or a manager: “I want to flag something. It may seem small, but it has been on my mind and I would like it noted.”
  • If you are not sure it counts: “I am not certain this is a POSH matter. Could we talk it through confidentially?” Asking is exactly what the routes are for.
  • If you noticed it happen to a colleague: “Hey, are you okay? That comment earlier seemed a little off to me.” A quiet, low-key check-in is a real action.

You can raise something that happened to you, or something you witnessed. Telling one trusted person is enough to start.

🗣️ Situation: The Messages That Drift

The situation: Aisha and a more senior member of the research team are coordinating a literature review. 🗣️ Situation: When It Becomes the Team’s Running Joke

The situation: In the team channel, one person keeps making affectionate remarks about a quieter colleague whenever she posts (“there she is, the star of the show”), always with a laughing emoji. Most of the group reacts with a laugh, so it has become routine and no one thinks of it as a problem. The colleague replies with a brief smiley each time, says little, and has started posting less and quietly skipping the optional calls.

What matters here: something becoming normal for the group does not make it welcome for the person it lands on. A polite emoji is not consent, and the quieter participation is a signal worth noticing. A teammate who simply does not pile on, and who checks in privately afterwards, can shift the whole tone. An RA or PA who sees it should address it early, as the person who sets the tone, rather than wait for a formal complaint.

📌 Key learning: normalisation is the trap. Normal for the group is never the same as welcome for the person it targets. Notice the quiet signals; they often speak before words do.
⚠️

Common pitfalls, the beliefs that keep good people silent

  • “I was only joking, so it is fine” → intention is not the test; if it was unwelcome, it counts, a joke included.
  • “Nothing explicit was said, so it is not harassment” → a pattern of personal attention qualifies on its own.
  • “It did not happen to me, so it is not my business” → you can raise what you witnessed; silence lets it become normal.
  • “If I report, the whole team will know” → confidentiality is built in; the person who hears it does not broadcast it.
  • “I have to be sure before I say anything” → you do not; when unsure, ask. The routes exist for exactly the ‘I am not certain’ moments.

Child Protection in the Field

Field deployments bring research staff into communities where children live, play, work, and sometimes suffer harm. In household surveys, children appear as household members, as bystanders during interviews, and occasionally as the subject of specific modules. Every interaction with a child, intentional or incidental, carries responsibility, because children cannot fully protect their own interests in the presence of adult strangers with institutional authority.

Core Principles, Do No Harm

PrincipleWhat It Requires in the Field
Never interview a child without specific IRB approval and parental consentChildren under 18 are not eligible respondents unless the study protocol specifically includes them with IRB-approved methods. Even then, a parent or guardian must consent and ideally be present.
Never be alone with a childNo one-on-one interactions with a child who is not one's own family member. This protects the child and protects field staff from any accusation of inappropriate conduct.
Never photograph a child without explicit parental consentEven incidental photographs, site photos, team photos, that include identifiable children require parental consent. Always ask before photographing.
Protect children's data with maximum confidentialityNames, ages, school enrollment, health status, or any other information about children collected in the survey is never shared outside the project team.
Social media: absolute zero tolerance for child imagesNo image of a child may be posted to any personal social media account, Instagram, WhatsApp status, Facebook, X, regardless of context. This is a termination-level violation.

When a Child Discloses Harm, The 4 Steps

  • Step 1, Stop probing. Do not ask follow-up questions about the harm. A surveyor is not a trained forensic interviewer. Additional questioning can contaminate future legal processes and re-traumatise the child.
  • Step 2, Listen without reacting dramatically. If the child or parent is speaking, listen. Do not minimise ("I'm sure it's not that bad") or dramatise ("We need to call the police right now"). A calm, neutral presence helps the child feel safe.
  • Step 3, Record verbatim. Write down exactly what was said or observed in the field diary. Time-stamped, word-for-word. Do not interpret or summarise at this stage.
  • Step 4, Escalate same day. Report to the PA or RA the same day. Do not decide alone whether it is "serious enough." The PA/RA decides in consultation with the RO Team Lead and the project's safeguarding focal point. The escalation decision is theirs, not the surveyor's.
📌 Important: If a child is observed in immediate physical danger, field staff may alert a nearby adult authority (police, teacher, health worker) in addition to the escalation chain, but should not take direct personal action beyond that. Ensure the safety of oneself and the team first.
🗣️ Situation: Never Interview a Minor Unsupervised

The situation: A surveyor reaches a household for an education survey and finds that only a young child is home; the parent, who is the eligible respondent, is out at work. The surveyor is tempted to ask the child a few roster questions while waiting, reasoning that it is quick and harmless.

The right step is to not enter and to not question the child at all. If the parent cannot be reached within a reasonable time, mark the household for a revisit and leave. No part of the survey, not even a household roster, is collected from a minor on their own.

📌 Key learning: “just a few questions” is never a reason to engage a minor without a guardian present. The rule exists to keep both the child and the surveyor safe. An extra revisit is a small price for getting child safeguarding right.

4.1 · SurveyCTO

SurveyCTO Navigation, Step by Step

Every surveyor must be able to open, complete, and sync a form confidently before their first field day. This walkthrough covers the core steps. Facilitator note: this section requires tablets in hand. Run through each step with the group doing it live.

1

Open SurveyCTO Collect

Tap the SurveyCTO Collect icon on the tablet home screen. The "Fill Blank Form" and "Edit Saved Form" options appear.

⚠️ Common error: Opening Google Chrome instead of SurveyCTO Collect. The icons look different, Collect has a clipboard logo.

2

Select the Correct Form Version

Tap "Fill Blank Form." A list of downloaded forms appears. Always check the form version date, if multiple versions are listed, use the most recent one.

⚠️ Common error: Selecting an outdated form version. Old versions may not contain updated skip patterns or question wording. If unsure, the surveyor should check with the supervisor before starting.

3

Read the Consent Screen, In Full

The form opens with the consent screen as the first page. Read this aloud to the respondent in their language before tapping "Next." Do not skip or rush the consent screen.

⚠️ Common error: Tapping "Next" on the consent screen before obtaining the respondent's agreement. This is a data integrity violation and an IRB breach.

4

GPS Capture, Wait for the Lock

When the form requests GPS, hold the tablet still and wait until the accuracy reaches ≤15 metres. If indoors, step outside for a moment. Never manually enter GPS coordinates.

⚠️ Common error: Tapping "Accept" on a GPS reading with 80+ metre accuracy. This makes the GPS verification layer in the QC system flag the form.

5

Hard Stops vs. Soft Flags

Hard stops (red): The form will not proceed until the error is corrected. Re-read the question and check the entry. Common causes: entering text in a numeric field, selecting an ineligible combination of answers.

Soft flags (yellow): The form allows the surveyor to proceed, but the entered answer is outside the expected range. Check with the respondent before continuing, do not simply ignore the flag.

6

Save and Sync, Before Leaving the Field

At end of the field day, go to "Send Finalized Form." Connect to WiFi or mobile data. Tap "Send Selected" or "Send All." Wait for the confirmation tick before leaving the network area.

⚠️ Common error: Returning to base without syncing. Forms stuck on "Finalized" overnight are a data quality risk, if the device is damaged or lost, those surveys are lost. Sync every day before leaving the field.

7

What to Do When Something Goes Wrong

Form crashes: Force close and re-open. The form auto-saves and usually reopens where it was left off. Battery dies mid-form: Charge and re-open. Do not start a new form, find the saved draft. Wrong respondent: Do not submit, mark as "Test/Error" in the survey ID field and flag to supervisor for deletion.

✅ The End-of-Day Sync Rule

No surveyor leaves the field with unsynchronised finalized forms. The supervisor must visually confirm sync completion (all forms show a green check) before allowing team departure. This is not optional, it is a daily accountability check that prevents data loss.

4.3 · Detection Architecture

The 5-Layer Fabrication Detection System

Every surveyor who has been caught fabricating believed they would not be caught. The detection architecture makes that belief false. These five layers operate simultaneously, data fabrication leaves traces across all of them.

L1

Duration Risk Review, Timestamp Analysis

SurveyCTO records the exact time from the first question to SUBMIT. A 35-minute survey completed in 8 minutes is statistically impossible to explain as a "fast respondent", especially when the same surveyor has 6 such forms in a day.

Detection reliability: Very High
L2

GPS Verification, Location Cross-Check

GPS coordinates captured in the form are cross-checked against the sample frame. Interviews claimed in Village X but GPS showing the surveyor was in town, at home, or at the field office are flagged immediately.

Detection reliability: High
L3

Back-Check, Re-Interview Verification

A monitor calls or re-visits 5 to 10% of completed surveys and asks the respondent a set of back-check questions. Discrepancies between the original survey and the back-check trigger a deeper investigation.

Detection reliability: Very High (when done correctly)
L4

Statistical Outlier Detection, Pattern Analysis

Automated analysis flags unusual patterns: identical response distributions across forms, implausible answer combinations, suspiciously low variance in numeric responses, or clusters of answers that don't match geographic or demographic expectations.

Detection reliability: Medium-High (catches systematic fabrication)
L5

Accompaniment & Spot Check, Live Observation

Supervisors and monitors conduct unannounced accompaniments (observing the full interview process) and spot checks (brief visits to confirm a household was actually visited and the respondent exists).

Detection reliability: Definitive for surveyors being checked
🗣️ Situation: A Completion Rate That Is Too Good (CATI)

The situation: An enumerator’s daily completion rate is 18 interviews a day when the team average is 11. A duration review shows the last six interviews each day averaging four minutes against an eighteen-minute design, and GPS shows the phone stationary for every call. A silent monitoring session confirms it: the enumerator dials, waits without connecting, and enters invented answers. They are removed the same day, and 47 interviews have to be collected again.

📌 Key learning: a suspiciously high completion rate is a red flag, not an achievement. Duration, GPS, and back-checks together make fabrication very hard to hide. The cost to the person is their place on the project; the cost to the project is time, money, and data quality. Neither is worth it.
4.4 · QC Architecture

Quality Control Infrastructure, Three Core Tools

1. Accompaniments

An accompaniment is when a supervisor or monitor observes the full survey process, from household approach through consent, interview, and form submission. This is the most powerful QC tool because it catches problems in real time.

ElementStandard
FrequencyMinimum 1 accompaniment per surveyor per week during active collection; daily during the first 3 days for new surveyors
Who conductsSupervisor for surveyors; PA/SC for supervisors; RA for PAs
What to observeConsent process (did they read the full script?), question reading accuracy (verbatim or paraphrased?), privacy (is the interview private?), response entry (entering what respondent says or interpreting?)
Feedback timingImmediate, after the interview, not at end of day. Delayed feedback is processed feedback and loses specificity.
FFR check"How many accompaniments have been conducted? What was documented?"

2. Spot Checks

A spot check is an unannounced brief visit to confirm that a survey actually happened, that the household was visited, the respondent exists, and basic facts match the form.

ElementStandard
SelectionRandom, not targeted at suspected fabricators (to maintain detection surprise)
Sample rate5 to 10% of completed surveys, minimum 3 per surveyor per week
Questions to askDid someone from [org] visit? What did they ask about? How long did it take? Did you sign something?
What triggers a deeper investigationRespondent unaware of visit; respondent description of survey duration dramatically different from recorded time; respondent denies specific modules that were recorded as completed

3. Back-Checks

A back-check re-administers a subset of questions from the original survey to the same respondent. It is designed to detect both fabrication (invented responses) and enumerator error (systematic misrecording).

ElementStandard
Selection5 to 10% of all completed surveys, randomly selected. Include at least 2 surveys per surveyor per week.
Question selectionChoose "stable" questions, those whose true answers don't change between interviews (e.g., household composition, asset ownership, respondent age). Avoid questions where recall naturally varies.
Acceptable discrepancy rateUnder 5%, if a surveyor's back-check discrepancy rate exceeds 10%, escalate to RA for investigation
DocumentationAll back-checks entered in back-check tracker with outcome: Pass / Minor Discrepancy / Major Discrepancy / Fabrication Suspected

🚩 The Ghost Access Problem

In several projects, access to tracking sheets with respondent PII remained active for months after fieldwork ended, because no one ran a deliberate access revocation process. Former surveyors could still open Google Sheets containing respondent names, phone numbers, and survey responses. This is a serious IRB concern. Data access revocation is a checklist task, done on Day 1 post-fieldwork, not a memory exercise.

Supervisors ask each surveyor three questions at midday: (1) How many surveys done this morning? (2) Any refusals or problems? (3) Do you need anything for the afternoon? 5.4 · Project Wrap-Up

Closing a Project Correctly, The Final Checklist

Project wrap-up is the most under-managed phase of field operations. When fieldwork ends, urgency fades, and important closure tasks get delayed or forgotten. Build the wrap-up checklist into the project plan from Day 1 so it has the same status as everything else.

  • All surveys synced and confirmed on SurveyCTO server, zero pending submissions verified by SC
  • Google Sheet access revoked for all field staff, only RA, SC, and RO retain access
  • SurveyCTO/ODK server password changed; all surveyor login credentials deactivated
  • All WhatsApp group chats and media cleared; field staff demoted from admin roles
  • All device data cleared: tablets factory reset; project phones wiped; personal phone supervised deletion session completed
  • Asset return scheduled: all field staff return all devices and accessories on a single day
  • Asset register updated: IMEI verified at return, condition documented, damage deductions processed per policy
  • Signed asset return acknowledgments collected from all staff
  • Consent forms: full count verified, stored at institutional office, access log maintained
  • Salary register finalised and signed by all staff for the final payment
  • Final project summary shared with RO Team Lead: survey count, quality summary, key learnings
  • Wrap-up checklist signed by RA and filed with RO Team Lead
🗣️ Situation: Wrap-up Is Part of the Job

The situation: Fieldwork for a self-help-group survey wraps in September. The RA leaves for a conference the same day, and the PA says he will “handle wrap-up.” Four months later, a routine check finds that 14 surveyors still have view access to a tracking sheet with 800 respondent names and numbers, three tablets were never returned, and the project phone still holds three months of coordination messages. No wrap-up checklist was ever completed.

The project needs a full wrap-up remediation and an incident notification, and a meeting with the PI to explain the gaps. The PA is put on an improvement plan, and the RA’s rating for the project notes weak wrap-up oversight.

📌 Key learning: wrap-up is not what happens after the interesting work. It protects participants, the organisation, and your own professional record. Build the checklist before fieldwork begins, and stay until it is done.
