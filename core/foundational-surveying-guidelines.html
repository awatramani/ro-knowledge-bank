1 · The Bigger Picture

Why This Work Matters

A Surveyor is not filling in a government form or running an opinion poll. A Surveyor generates scientific evidence, and the standards are scientific, not administrative.

🔑 KEY PRINCIPLE
Every number recorded, every response captured, every GPS point taken is a brick in the wall of evidence that governments and organisations use to decide how to spend limited resources on the people who need help most. If the brick is crooked, the wall is weak, and the wrong decision gets made about health, education, or poverty programs. 

Governments and NGOs spend thousands of crores every year on programs meant to reduce poverty, improve schooling, raise vaccination, or expand financial access. Many are well-intentioned, but without rigorous evidence there is no way to know whether they actually work. Some programs that look impressive on paper turn out to do nothing, or even cause harm at scale. The job of a field team is to collect the evidence that separates what works from what does not.

What J-PAL Does, in Plain Terms: Your FYI

J-PAL works to find out which programmes and policies actually help people, so that limited resources go where they do the most good. The main way it does this is a randomised evaluation (often called an RCT). Surveyors and field staff do not need the technical depth, just a simple, accurate picture, enough to represent the work with confidence and to answer a respondent's honest question.

The idea in one breath

Think of a fair lottery. From a group of eligible villages, households, or schools, some are chosen at random to receive a program (the treatment group) and others continue as usual for now (the comparison group). Because the choice is random, the two groups start out alike in every way, so any difference measured later can be credited to the program itself, not to luck or pre-existing advantages. The surveys are how that difference is measured.

How much should a Surveyor explain to a respondent?

Keep it simple and broadly build around whatever the consent script covers, the study's general topic and the partner organization, plus a plain-language sense of who J-PAL is. That is enough. A Surveyor never needs to explain study design, group assignment, or analysis, and never speculates about results or who will receive a program. "We are a research organization studying how families here manage [topic], so program can be designed to help better" is the right level.

Why every respondent matters, including the comparison group

The comparison group is exactly as important as the treatment group. Survey one more carefully than the other, or let more comparison-group respondents drop out, and the comparison breaks. That is why each sampled respondent, easy or hard to reach, treatment or comparison, receives the same care and the same effort.

🗣️ Scenario, The farmer who asks: "Why wasn't I chosen?"

During a baseline in Madhya Pradesh a farmer asks why his village was not selected for the new seed variety. A good answer: "The villages in this study were chosen by a method like a lottery, random selection. It has nothing to do with the size of your village. To understand whether the programme works, we need information from villages that receive it and villages that do not. Your participation is extremely valuable either way." It is honest, respectful, promises nothing, and affirms the respondent's value.

📌 The response never promises future inclusion and never apologizes for the design; it explains randomization in plain language and dignifies the respondent's contribution.

🔑 The Golden Rule of Field Judgment
When in doubt, do not guess, call the supervisor. There is no shame in asking and no penalty for calling. The only mistake is making a decision you were not authorised to make and not telling anyone. A wrong decision reported immediately can almost always be corrected; one that is hidden gets discovered later and is far harder to fix.

5 · Reporting

What to Report, to Whom, and How Fast

Reporting is not paperwork for its own sake, it is how problems surface while they are still small. Three documents carry a Surveyor's professional record: the daily activity log, field notes, and incident reports.

The Daily Activity Log, every workday, no exceptions

Filled at the end of every field day, the daily log is the Surveyor's professional record and the project's audit trail. It captures: date and Surveyor ID; location; interviews attempted vs. completed; refusals; callbacks scheduled; travel and fieldwork hours; devices used; whether data was synced; and any issues or observations.

Field Notes, write them immediately after each interview

✅ Useful field notes
    • "Household appears wealthier than reported income suggests, pucca house, motorcycle, satellite dish. Income likely under-reported."
    • "There were a few questions that respondent completely refused to answer."

🚩 Useless field notes
  • "Interview done. No issues."
  • "Normal household."
  • "Respondent answered all questions."

The defensive response ("the system is wrong, my data is fine") helps no one.

6 · Professional Conduct

How to Carry Yourself, and the Integrity Line

For the respondent, the Surveyor is the study. Appearance, language, punctuality, and behavior form the respondent's entire impression of the research and the organization and that impression decides whether they trust the Surveyor with honest answers.

👤

Appearance

Dress modestly and appropriately for the context, never signaling wealth or authority. Wear the ID badge visibly. Remove footwear before entering homes where that is the norm, without being asked.

💬

Communication

Greet first, in the local greeting. Speak the respondent’s preferred language. Never use jargon. Never argue with a respondent, record what they say, do not correct it. Stay professionally neutral on politics, religion, caste, and schemes.

⏱️

Reliability

Arrive on time, ten minutes early for an appointment. If delayed, inform the respondent as early as possible. Meet daily targets without cutting corners; if consistently behind, raise workload with the supervisor rather than rushing interviews.

On the road, non-negotiable riding safety

A helmet and proper closed shoes are mandatory for any staff member driving or riding a motorcycle to or between field sites, every trip, no exceptions, including the rider and any pillion. Ride within speed limits, never on a phone, and never after dark on unfamiliar roads. Reaching a household safely always comes before reaching it quickly.

Grounds for immediate termination, no second chances

  • Data fabrication, filling in responses without conducting the interview. It does not matter if the house was far, the respondent rude, or the day long.
  • Data falsification, changing a respondent's answer to "make sense," pass a validation check, or avoid a flag.
  • Interviewing the wrong respondent and recording it under the listed respondent's ID.
  • Sharing respondent data with anyone outside the research team.
  • Accepting gifts, payment, or favours from respondents in exchange for favourable treatment.
  • Using the survey device for personal purposes; social media, photos, calls, entertainment.

What integrity looks like in the field

  • Marking a household "respondent absent" when they are truly absent, even though it means coming back tomorrow.
  • Recording "Don't Know" when the respondent genuinely does not know, rather than guessing a plausible answer.
  • Reporting a questionnaire problem even though it slows the day down.
  • Telling a respondent honestly that you do not know the answer to their question, rather than inventing one.

1 · Foundations

The Four Pillars of Ethical Research

Research ethics is not abstract theory, it is a set of concrete, non-negotiable rules that govern behaviour every minute in the field. These rules exist because of a painful history of research that harmed participants.

Pillar 1, Do No Harm (Non-Maleficence)

A Surveyor's presence in a respondent's life must never make their situation worse, not emotionally, socially, physically, or economically. Harm in the field is rarely dramatic; it is usually subtle: a woman punished by in-laws for answering about her husband's drinking within earshot; a farmer resented by neighbours who assume he is getting benefits; an adolescent humiliated by a menstrual-hygiene question asked in front of her father and brothers. Before every sensitive question, evaluate the environment. If answering could bring consequences, adjust the setting, defer the module, or skip it and document why.

Pillar 2, Respect for Persons (Autonomy)

Every person has an absolute right to decide, freely and without pressure, whether to participate. The respondent's "no" is final, no negotiation, no guilt, no implied consequences. They may withdraw at any point, even at the last question. Every respondent receives identical dignity regardless of caste, gender, religion, wealth, education, or appearance.

Pillar 3, Beneficence (Maximising Good)

While never causing harm, strive to generate the greatest good, which for a Surveyor means one thing: collect the highest-quality data possible. Good data → accurate findings → good policy → improved lives. The chain from a clipboard to someone's better life is real, and it runs through data quality. Beneficence also means a human obligation to assist or call for help in a genuine medical emergency, a life-threatening situation outranks the survey.

Pillar 4, Justice (Fairness)

The burdens and benefits of research must be shared fairly. Do not over-survey easy-to-reach households while neglecting hard ones, every sampled household deserves equal effort. Behave identically in every household. And approach communities that have historically been treated as "research subjects" without benefit, particularly poor, marginalised, and tribal populations, with particular sensitivity, transparency, and respect.

2 · Oversight

The Ethics Committee (IRB), What It Means for a Surveyor

Every legitimate study with human subjects is reviewed and approved by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) / Ethics Committee before any fieldwork is permitted. Its job is to ensure the study will not harm participants.

Five things a Surveyor can never do

  • Cannot modify the consent process. The script is approved exactly as written, do not add, subtract, or paraphrase in ways that change its meaning.
  • Cannot add questions. If a question seems missing, tell the supervisor; never ask it on your own. Unapproved questions have not been reviewed for ethical risk.
  • Cannot skip consent, even if the respondent says "just get on with it." It is a legal and ethical requirement.
  • Cannot share data with anyone outside the research team.
  • Violations have consequences. Improper consent or unauthorised data sharing can shut down the entire study. Responsible individuals face immediate termination and possible legal action.
3 · The Process

Informed Consent, A Complete Operational Guide

Informed consent is not a signed form, it is a process through which a respondent reaches a genuine, uncoerced decision to participate after understanding what it involves. The form merely documents the process.

Stage 1, Before consent: approach, introduction & environment

Consent begins before a word is spoken, in how the household is approached and the setting chosen. Obtain consent where the respondent can think, ask questions, and decide without social pressure: not amid watching neighbours, not at a chaotic doorstep, not in the presence of a leader who "brought you," but ideally somewhere relatively private; inside, under a tree, on a courtyard cot.

A clean introduction template

"Namaste. My name is [name]. I am a surveyor working with [organisation]. We are conducting a research study in this area to understand [one-sentence topic]. I'd like to explain the study and, if you are willing, ask whether you'd like to participate. May I take a few minutes to explain?", It names you and the organisation, states the topic without jargon, asks permission even to explain, and promises nothing.

Stage 2, During consent: the seven essential elements

The exact words need not match the form, but the meaning of each element must be clearly conveyed and understood:

#ElementWhat to communicate (in plain words)
1PurposeWhat the study is about and why, the one-sentence summary from training; no jargon.
2What participation involvesWhat the respondent will do, for roughly how long, on what topics. Be honest about duration, if it is 45 minutes, say 45 minutes.
3Voluntary participationEntirely their choice. They can say no now or stop anytime. No penalty. Access to government services or benefits is unaffected.
4Risks & discomfortSome questions may be personal. Any question may be skipped, with no consequence.
5ConfidentialityEverything is strictly confidential. Their name will not appear in any report; answers are combined with thousands of others and analysed as a group.
6No direct benefitParticipation guarantees no money, service, or programme enrolment. The research may improve programmes in future, but there is no immediate personal benefit.
7Contact informationWhom to contact with questions or complaints, supervisor name/number or organisation helpline, ideally on a printed card.

After explaining all seven, invite questions, wait, answer honestly, then ask: "Do you agree to participate?" Consent may be documented by signature, thumbprint (with an independent witness), audio-recorded verbal consent, or a CAPI checkbox, as the protocol specifies.

✅ What good consent looks like
  • The Surveyor sits, introduces herself, reads each element slowly, and pauses. When the respondent looks unsure and asks about confidentiality she explains: "This means if you tell me your income is ₹5,000, nobody, not your neighbour, not the sarpanch, not the government, will ever see that number attached to your name." Asked "will I still get my pension?" she answers "Absolutely, your pension has nothing to do with this survey." Time taken: 6 minutes.
🚩 A red-flag consent process
  • The Surveyor says "the sarpanch sent me, I need to ask some questions for a government survey, it won't take long," hands over the form and says "just sign here." The respondent, who cannot read, signs. The survey begins immediately. Time taken: 30 seconds.

In the red-flag version the respondent did not give informed consent: they did not understand what they agreed to, were misled about who sent the Surveyor, and may have felt coerced by the mention of the sarpanch. The interview is ethically invalid, and if discovered, the data cannot be used.

🌼 A little field story

A Surveyor in Maharashtra spent six unhurried minutes on consent with an elderly farmer who had turned away three other surveys that month. At the end the farmer smiled and said, “You are the first one who asked me, not told me.” He gave a thoughtful, complete interview, and insisted on chai afterwards. Good consent is not a delay; it is the start of good data.

4 · Edge Cases

Special Consent Scenarios

The textbook scenario, a literate adult, in private, freely choosing, is the exception in Indian fieldwork, not the rule.

ScenarioAdditional protocol
Minors (under 18)Two consents are required: parental/guardian consent (all seven elements) and the minor's assent in age-appropriate language. If either is missing, the interview does not proceed.
Limited literacyRead the entire form aloud in the respondent's language at a pace they can follow; pause after each section to check understanding. Never ask someone to sign what they cannot read, use thumbprint or audio-recorded verbal consent.


Third parties presentThe most common challenge. Politely use the organisation-rule framing to request privacy; suggest a practical arrangement (verandah, shade behind the house). Never disrespect or forcibly remove a family elder.
🗣️ Scenario, The mother-in-law in the room (Haryana)

During a women's health and decision-making survey, the listed respondent is 24-year-old Sunita; her mother-in-law moves closer: "What kind of questions? Let me hear." The correct response acknowledges the elder respectfully, "Namaste, Aunty-ji, thank you for welcoming me. This survey is meant to hear from Sunita directly, about her individual experience. It would help if I could speak with her privately for about 40 minutes, I'm happy to explain the general topic to you, but the questions need to be answered by Sunita alone." If the mother-in-law insists on staying, do not force it: suggest returning when Sunita can be private, or complete only the non-sensitive sections and flag the sensitive modules (decision-making, financial control, safety) for a private re-visit.

📌 If Sunita answers decision-making questions with her mother-in-law listening, she will give the socially acceptable answer, not the true one, and may face consequences after the Surveyor leaves. Privacy is both an ethics and a data-quality requirement.
🌼 A little field story

In a Tamil Nadu village, a respondent feared the audio recording was “for the police.” The Surveyor calmly explained what it was really for and offered, genuinely, to record nothing at all. Reassured by being given a real choice, the respondent not only finished the interview but walked the Surveyor to two neighbours’ homes. Respecting a worry often opens more doors than answering it.

Never do any of these in response to a refusal

  • "But the sarpanch said everyone should participate." (authority coercion)
  • "It will only take 10 minutes." (when it takes 45, dishonest)
  • "Everyone else has already done it." (social pressure)
  • "If you don't participate, we can't help your village." (implied consequence)
  • Returning to a household that refused, without explicit supervisor authorisation. One "no" is final.
6 · The Trust

Confidentiality & Data Protection

When a respondent participates, they entrust personal information about their life, income, health, family, fears. That trust is sacred; breaching it can have real consequences for the respondent.

☑️ What confidentiality means in practice
  • Never discuss one respondent's answers with another respondent, ever, not even as an example.
  • Never share data with leaders, officials, NGO workers, family, or anyone outside the research team, however politely they ask, whatever authority they claim.
  • Never leave the device unlocked and unattended, lock the screen even for a 30-second step away.
  • Refer to respondents by ID number, not name, in all team discussions and reports.
  • Never photograph respondents or their documents (Aadhaar, ration card, land records) unless the protocol requires it and consent was obtained.
  • Never post field experiences on social media in any way that could identify a respondent, location, or community.
⚡ If this, then that
IF a village leader says "show me what people are saying about our village."
THEN "I'm not able to share individual responses, all data is confidential and managed by our organisation. I can describe the study's general purpose, but not what any individual told me."
IF a police officer or official demands the data or device.
THEN Stay calm and polite. Explain it is confidential academic research you are not authorised to share; offer the supervisor's number and the organisation's letter. Do not physically resist; if forced, comply and call the supervisor immediately afterwards.
IF a family member asks "what did she tell you about our family?"
THEN "I'm not able to share what any individual told me, our conversations are private. I can explain the general topics, but not anyone's specific answers."

Device & data handling

Use a PIN or password lock (not an easily-observed pattern). Keep personal apps off survey devices. Submit completed surveys as soon as connectivity allows, data sitting on a device overnight is data at risk. Report a lost or stolen device immediately so the team can attempt a remote wipe. When the study ends, the team performs a full wipe, never attempt to delete data yourself.

1 · The Frame

Understanding the Sampling Frame

Before a single household is visited, the research team spends weeks building a sampling frame, the complete list of all potential participants from which the study sample is drawn. Think of it as the universe from which lottery tickets are created.

Frames come from household-listing data, government registers (voter rolls, BPL/SECC lists, school or PHC registers), programme enrolment records, or custom screening. The frame determines who is surveyed and, equally important, who is not.

🔑 KEY PRINCIPLE
The sample is sacred. You did not choose it; your supervisor did not choose it. It was generated by a statistical process so that findings can be generalised to the whole target population. Every deviation, every substitution, every skip, every convenience replacement, weakens that generalisability. You cannot add people. You cannot substitute people. You cannot skip people because they are inconvenient.
2 · Listing

Household Listing & Census Operations

In many studies the first activity is not surveying but listing, visiting every dwelling in a defined area and recording basic information that becomes the sampling frame. Errors here cascade through every later phase: miss a household and it has zero chance of selection.

DimensionRural villageUrban slum / peri-urban
Dwelling boundariesGenerally clear, distinct structures, often with compound wallsAmbiguous, multiple families per structure; rooms as separate households; lane numbering often absent
Household definitionJoint families common, clarify shared vs. separate cooking carefullyExtreme density, each unique cooking arrangement is one household
NavigationLandmarks: temple, school, panchayat, water tank; GPS usefulNarrow irregular lanes, no formal addresses; sketch maps and GPS essential (accuracy limited)
Mobility / turnoverLow to moderate; seasonal migration but a permanent baseHigh, frequent moves; whole clusters can be demolished between rounds; tracking is the greatest challenge

The listing protocol, step by step

1. Define and walk the perimeter of the assigned area before starting. 2. Choose a fixed starting point and a systematic path, door to door, lane by lane; never criss-cross or skip-and-return. 3. At each dwelling, identify all households using the standard definition: a group who normally live together and share a common kitchen. Two families cooking separately in one house = two households. 4. Record every field, head's name, spouse, member counts, GPS, address description with landmarks, and a phone number if available (a missing number now is one fewer way to track this household later). 5. Mark the dwelling with the listing ID if the protocol requires. 6. Capture a GPS waypoint at the main entrance, waiting for accuracy below the protocol threshold.

🗣️ Scenario, Listing a relocated urban slum (Ahmedabad)

A resettlement colony of identical concrete blocks in grid rows, no painted house numbers; some structures hold multiple families across floors, some are vacant, some are shops. How to handle: create a numbering system (Row A, Structure 1, 2…); chalk-mark each in the agreed position; knock on every door and ask "how many families live here? Does each cook separately?", recording each cooking unit as a household; record vacant structures as "Vacant" with a reason; record shops as "Non-residential" unless someone also lives there; and capture GPS at every entrance (in a grid, GPS alone won't distinguish adjacent units, the chalk numbering is essential).

3 · Verification

Confirming Identity & Eligibility

The assignment sheet carries identifying details, name, household head, address, phone, sometimes age, spouse, or number of children. Confirm identity through natural conversation before beginning.

⚡ If this, then that
IF the head's name matches but the listed respondent is absent.
THEN Ask where they are and when they return; schedule a callback. Do NOT interview a different member unless the protocol explicitly allows a proxy.
IF a different family now lives at the address.
THEN The original household may have moved. Ask the current occupants for forwarding information; mark "Migrated" with details; report to supervisor.
IF the name matches but key details don't (age off by 15 years, different spouse).
THEN It may be a different person with the same name. Verify additional details; if identity can't be confirmed with confidence, do NOT proceed, record the discrepancy and escalate.
IF two houses both claim to be the right one.
THEN Use secondary identifiers (head's name, phone, listing GPS). If still unclear, do NOT guess, call the supervisor.
⚠️ Never make eligibility decisions alone

Some studies verify eligibility at survey time (e.g. a child still under 24 months; currently pregnant; never had a bank account). Complete the screening section honestly; if the respondent is ineligible, record it and stop, do not proceed just to hit a target. If a case is ambiguous (e.g. a woman who had a miscarriage, is she still a "pregnant woman"?), mark "Eligibility Unclear" and escalate. The research team decides.

⚠️ The minimum three-attempt rule

Make a minimum of three contact attempts at different times of day and on different days before using any non-completion code (04, 06, 07, 08). One visit at 2 PM on a Tuesday teaches almost nothing, a farmer is in the field by morning and home by evening; a labourer may only be home on Sunday. Every attempt is logged: date, time, who was contacted, outcome, next action. The log is the proof of genuine effort, and it protects the Surveyor.

6 · The Iron Rule

Replacement Protocols

Replacement is the last resort, not the first response, and it is never the Surveyor's decision.

⚠️ Critical, The Replacement Rule
A Surveyor NEVER chooses a replacement respondent. Never. Under any circumstance. The decision to replace, and the selection of the replacement, is made by the research team through a predefined statistical process. The Surveyor's role is to document why the original cannot be surveyed, report to the supervisor, and wait for instructions.

The process: (1) exhaust all tracking efforts, ≥3 attempts at different times, phone calls, neighbour inquiries, checks with the AWC/school/PHC; (2) supervisor reviews the contact-attempt log and confirms the respondent is non-contactable; (3) the research manager decides whether a replacement is warranted; (4) if approved, the replacement is drawn from a pre-generated replacement list created by the same random process; (5) everything is documented.

✅ Correct
  • Visit Geeta Devi's house three times; she's never home; neighbours say she's at her parents' for a month; log all three attempts and the neighbour's information; call her number twice (no answer); submit to the supervisor as "Temporarily Migrated (07)"; the supervisor schedules a callback in four weeks.
🚩 Termination-level violation
  • Visit once at 11 AM; she's not home; interview her neighbour with "similar characteristics" and record it under Geeta Devi's ID. "Nobody will know", wrong. High-frequency checks flag duration and GPS anomalies; the back-check calls "Geeta Devi" and the neighbour answers. Termination follows.
7 · The Foundation

Tracking Information, Collect More, Not Less

At baseline, the tracking information collected now is the single biggest predictor of attrition at midline and endline. Invest the time.

☑️ Tracking information to collect at every visit
  • Full name (as locally known + any formal/document name); father's or husband's name; age / approximate DOB
  • Phone 1 (primary), Phone 2 (secondary/spouse/relative), and an alternate contact person with relationship & number
  • GPS of the dwelling + a detailed landmark-based address description + ward/mohalla/tola name + nearest recognised landmark
  • Does the household migrate seasonally? Where and when? Permanent/native village address if different
  • Name & number of a relative at the permanent village; name & number of an employer or labour contractor (sardar/thekedaar)
  • A neighbour who knows the family well; the local ASHA/AWW/ANM for health studies

The golden rule of tracking information

If in doubt, collect more. An extra phone number that turns out unnecessary costs ten seconds. A missing number that could have located a migrated respondent costs the study a data point worth weeks of effort.

1 · Architecture

Understanding the Survey Instrument

A questionnaire is not a random list of questions, it is an engineered data-collection architecture. Every module, skip pattern, and validation rule exists because a researcher needs a specific piece of information. Understanding it turns a question-reader into a professional who can anticipate, troubleshoot, and flag genuine problems.

TermDefinitionExample
ModuleA thematic block of questions on one topicModule 3: Household Agriculture, crops, inputs, land
Skip pattern (relevance)Logic that hides/shows questions based on prior answersIf "own land?" = No → skip all land-measurement questions
ConstraintA rule that rejects invalid answersAge 0 to 120; income cannot be negative
Repeat group / rosterA set of questions asked for each member of a listFor each household member: name, age, gender, education, occupation

Trust the skip patterns, and master the roster

When the form skips a block, it is working correctly, do not panic, go back, or force-answer skipped questions. If a skip seems wrong, verify the triggering question first; nine times in ten the error is in the trigger answer, not the logic.

Rosters are where most data-quality errors occur. Before starting one, confirm the complete list: "Please tell me the name of every person who normally lives and eats in this household, starting with the head." Count the total, then begin, and never add or remove members mid-roster without reconfirming.

☑️ Pre-flight check, before starting any new form
  • Device charged above 50% (or a power bank available)
  • Correct form version loaded (check the version number against the briefing)
  • Date & time correct (auto-sync enabled)
  • GPS / location services ON
  • At least 500 MB free storage
  • Previous completed forms submitted / synced
  • Correct household ID / respondent assignment in hand
2 · The Workflow

Filling a Digital Form, Start to Finish

Whatever app the project uses, the logic is the same: download a blank form, fill it during the interview, and submit the finalized form to the server. Master the mechanics so completely that the technology becomes invisible and full attention stays on the respondent.

Step 1, Download & verify. Get the blank form from the server; open it once to confirm it loads; check the version number against what the supervisor announced. Never begin with an outdated version, answers on a wrong version may have to be discarded.
Step 2, Start. Enter household identification carefully; the ID is the only link between the data and the sampled household. Read it back to yourself; use barcode scan if available.
Step 3, Pause & resume. If the respondent must step away, save as a draft immediately; never leave a form open and idle. On resuming, re-read the last two or three answers to re-establish context.
Step 4, Finalize & submit. Review the summary screen, finalize, and submit as soon as connectivity allows; confirm the status shows "Sent" / a green tick, not "Pending" or "Failed."

☑️ End-of-interview submission checklist
  • All mandatory fields filled (no constraint errors remaining)
  • Household ID and respondent name match the assignment sheet
  • GPS coordinates captured (non-zero values)
  • Form is FINALIZED, not saved as draft
  • Form is SUBMITTED successfully (status checked)

Submission errors, what they mean

  • "Server rejected": outdated form version or wrong server URL, contact the supervisor.
  • "No internet": expected in rural areas; forms are stored safely on the device, sync at a connectivity zone.
  • "Constraint violated": an invalid answer, find the question with the red error.
  • "Storage full": delete photos/apps; NEVER delete CAPI application data.
  • Forms stuck "Pending" >24h: escalate, possible server-side issue.
🗣️ Scenario, Form-version mismatch mid-day (Jharkhand)

Three interviews in, the supervisor messages: "version 4.2 pushed, delete 4.1 and re-download." The two already submitted on 4.1 are on the server; the data team handles migration, no need to redo them. The in-progress draft on 4.1: check with the supervisor, if changes are minor, finalize and submit as-is; if structural (skip logic changed, questions reordered), note the household ID, discard the draft, and revisit with 4.2. Download 4.2, verify it loads, resume, and log the affected IDs.

GPS capture, getting it right

GPS accuracy is the location-truthfulness score that back-check teams use to verify a visit. 1 · The Bridge

Building Rapport Across Diverse Communities

Rapport is the invisible bridge between Surveyor and respondent. Without it, answers are polite but hollow; with it, the data reflects reality. Rapport is not about being friendly, it is about being credible, respectful, and genuinely interested.

The first 90 seconds, the make-or-break window

Respondents decide within roughly 90 seconds whether they will cooperate genuinely or merely tolerate the Surveyor. Use them well: (1) Approach calmly, wait to be noticed or call a greeting rather than entering uninvited. (2) Introduce name, organisation, and purpose in simple language, never "randomized controlled trial" or "impact evaluation," but "we are studying how families here manage their farming/health/children's schooling, and your household was selected." (3) Establish legitimacy, show the ID card unasked. (4) Be honest about duration, under-promising "just 5 minutes" then taking 45 destroys trust for every Surveyor who follows.

A PVTG village head says flatly: "How many more surveys? People came last year too, nothing happened." Approach: acknowledge the frustration honestly ("You are right, many people come and you feel nothing changes; I understand"); differentiate the work ("Ours is only to understand how families here live, this is not a government survey and we are not giving any scheme; but your experiences help determine what works and what does not"); ask permission rather than demand compliance ("Can you spare an hour? If work comes up, we can stop and return"). Never promise outcomes, you do not control policy.

✅ What good rapport looks like
  • Respondent makes eye contact, asks clarifying questions, shares anecdotes
  • Offers chai/water (a strong signal of acceptance in rural India)
  • Says "let me think" before answering, genuine engagement
  • Voluntarily corrects an earlier answer: "Wait, I said 2 bigha but it's 1.5"
🚩 Signs rapport has failed
  • One-word answers, avoids eye contact
  • Keeps checking the time or the door
  • Defers every answer to an absent family member
  • "Whatever you want to write, write", passive non-cooperation
2 · The Toolkit

Probing, Neutral Probing, Laddering, Silence

Probing draws out a complete, accurate answer when the first response is vague, without putting words in the respondent's mouth. The difference between a probe and a leading question is the difference between good data and garbage: a probe asks "can you tell me more?"; a leading question asks "so you earn about 10,000, right?" One illuminates; the other contaminates.

The golden rules of probing

1. Never suggest an answer, draw out theirs, don't provide one. 2. Never show surprise, approval, or disapproval, face and tone stay neutral. 3. On "I don't know," probe once ("take your time, even an estimate helps"); if still unknown, record "Don't Know" and move on, do not badger. 4. For pre-coded questions, probe to classify, not to change the answer; if their reality fits no category, note it in comments.

🗣️ Scenario, The shopkeeper who answers "thik-thak hai" (Varanasi)

Every financial question gets a deflecting "it's okay / expenses happen." Probing sequence: (1) specification with wide, non-threatening ranges, "closer to ₹5,000, ₹10,000, ₹20,000, or something different?"; (2) anchor on a less sensitive daily figure, "on an average day, roughly how much do you sell?"; (3) normalise & reassure, "many shopkeepers here have shared similar figures; this is completely confidential and never goes to any tax authority"; (4) if still declined, record "Refused" and note the probing, never fabricate or estimate on their behalf. Never say "surely you earn at least 15,000?" or cite a neighbour's figure, both lead and breach confidentiality.

✅ Neutral probing
  • "Can you tell me more about that?"
  • "What do you mean by [local term]?"
  • "Take your time, there is no right or wrong answer"
  • Offering the full range of options without emphasis
🚩 Leading / biased probing
  • "So you probably earn around 10,000, right?"
  • "Most people here use government hospitals, you do too, don't you?"
  • "That much? Are you sure?" (expressing surprise)
  • Nodding at some answers and frowning at others
3 · The Hardest Questions

Handling Sensitive Topics

Income, caste, religion, health, reproductive health, domestic violence, substance use, illegal activity, where the greatest data-quality risks and the greatest ethical responsibilities collide.

🔑 KEY PRINCIPLE
If there is ever a conflict between getting a data point and protecting a respondent, the respondent wins. Every time. No exceptions.

Income, assets & finances

Respondents hesitate fearing taxation, loss of BPL/ration status, neighbour jealousy, or scheme-eligibility checks. Open the module with a normalising transition ("many families find these questions ordinary, we ask everyone the same and your answers are completely confidential"); use ranges where the form allows; and ask about each income source separately, farmers forget livestock, daily wage, MGNREGA, and remittances.

🗣️ Scenario, Under-reported income (Muzaffarpur, Bihar)

A labourer reports ₹2,000 to 3,000/month, but there's a motorcycle, a TV antenna, and children in private-school uniforms. Do: never contradict ("but you have a motorcycle?"); probe each source individually (days of work × daily wage; other earners; remittances) and let the form total it; reinforce confidentiality ("this has nothing to do with your ration card"); if the lower figure stands, record it and comment "possible under-reporting, visible asset mismatch; sources probed individually." Never change an answer to what you believe is more accurate, record what they tell you, not what you think.

Caste, religion & identity

Frame it as research ("we ask everyone the same question to understand how different communities experience [topic]; your answer is confidential and never linked to your name"). If asked "why do you need my caste?", explain without defensiveness and offer to mark it declined. Never guess or infer caste or religion from surname, appearance, or location, if declined, record "Refused."

Domestic violence & safety modules, highest protocol

Four non-negotiables for DV / safety modules

1. Mandatory isolation, the respondent must be completely alone (no husband, mother-in-law, or children old enough to understand). If privacy is impossible, skip the module and note the reason, collecting DV data in front of the perpetrator is not just bad data, it is dangerous. 2. Gender-matched interviewing, ideally a female Surveyor for a female respondent; otherwise the male Surveyor must have trauma-informed training. 3. Safety preamble, "these questions are about your personal safety; they are asked of women across the country; if at any point you want to stop, we stop immediately; everything stays confidential." 4. Referral afterwards, regardless of answers, provide the general services card (women's helpline 181, one-stop centre, protection officer), designed to look like a generic health-and-services card.

🗣️ Scenario, The young woman who goes silent (Anand, Gujarat)

At the empowerment module, asked who decides spending, Bhavna glances at the door, whispers "it is all in his hands," and falls silent. Response: do not push, "It's okay, there's no rush; these questions are asked to everyone"; offer to skip, "would you like to skip this section? That's completely fine"; if she agrees, mark "respondent chose not to answer" and move on; provide the general services card casually at the end; never discuss her reaction with villagers or family; and note in field notes: "respondent distressed at empowerment module; skipped at her request; referral card provided."

4 · Stamina

Managing Respondent Fatigue in Long Surveys

Many evaluations run 60 to 120 minutes. Fatigue is a systematic data-quality threat: tired respondents give shorter, less accurate, more socially-desirable answers, round numbers, default to "don't know," and rush the final modules, often the most analytically important.

Prevention

Signpost where they are ("we've finished the household section; agriculture is the largest, about 20 minutes left"), knowing there is an endpoint reduces anxiety. Keep a conversational pace with natural transitions, not a robotic read. Take strategic breaks after ~40 minutes; in rural India a chai break resets the dynamic (accept chai, refusing can feel like rejecting hospitality). If they flag and a less-critical optional module remains, consult the supervisor about deprioritising it.

🗣️ Scenario, The 90-minute survey and the evening cattle (Warangal, Telangana)

55 minutes in, a 70-year-old respondent needs to tie up the cows before dark. Do not rush the remaining 35 minutes, rushed answers are useless. Instead: "You've been very helpful, please tend to the cattle; I'll come back tomorrow at 9 AM, would that work?" Save as a draft, note the last completed question, and on return re-read the last two or three answers to re-orient. Log the split and the reason.

In tribal areas an interpreter introduces a quality risk, they may summarise or editorialise. 6 · Privacy

Conducting Interviews in the Presence of Others

In joint families, one-room dwellings, and dense settlements, privacy is a luxury. Others present introduce influence bias, social-desirability bias, and response suppression. The job is to maximise privacy where possible and document its absence where not.

How to request privacy, respectfully

"For this interview we need to speak with [name] alone, it is a requirement of our survey. Would you mind if we sat separately?" Then suggest a specific arrangement: "Can we sit on the verandah?" or "in the shade behind the house?" Authority over research protocol never overrides social courtesy, never argue with, disrespect, or forcibly remove a family elder.

7 · Their Words

Recording Verbatim for Open-Ended Questions

Open-ended questions exist because the researcher needs the respondent's own words, their framing, vocabulary, reasoning. Paraphrasing replaces the respondent's voice with the Surveyor's and makes the data useless for analysis.

Ask them to speak at a comfortable pace ("I'm writing this down, so please go a little slowly"); type/write as they speak (standard abbreviations are fine, but never abbreviate the substance); read it back ("you said this, did I understand correctly?"); write in the local language first if needed, adding a bracketed translation if the project requires; and include meaningful hesitations and self-corrections, omitting only random filler.

✅ Good verbatim recording
  • "Bachche ko sarkari school mein bheja kyunki private ki fees ki haisiyat nahin hai, warna private mein padhaate"
  • "Fasal barbaad ho gayi, baarish nahin aayi, karz bhi lena pada kisaan credit card se"
  • "Panchayat jaate hain lekin sunwai nahin hoti, sarpanch apne logon ka kaam pehle karta hai"
🚩 Paraphrased / summarised
  • "Cannot afford private school" (loses the reasoning and emotion)
  • "Crop failure due to drought" (loses the loan-source detail)
  • "Dissatisfied with panchayat" (loses the specific favouritism complaint)
🗣️ Scenario, The Sarpanch's rapid-fire 3-minute answer (Rajasthan)

Asked the village's biggest challenges, the Sarpanch covers roads, water, schools, the understaffed PHC, out-migration, and a land dispute in one breath. Let him speak 30 to 60 seconds to grasp the scope; at a pause, "you're making important points, I want to write everything; can we go through each one so I note it accurately?"; capture key phrases verbatim ("the road is so bad even an ambulance can't come in the rains"); read back the full response; and if text space is limited, prioritise the respondent's exact words over your summary, using the comments field for overflow.

The Do's and Don'ts of Surveying

The whole craft, distilled.

✅ Always
  • Greet first, in the local greeting; show the ID unasked
  • Read every question exactly as written, at the respondent's pace
  • Probe neutrally; offer the full range of options
  • Keep a flat, warm face, no surprise, approval, or disapproval
  • Ensure privacy before sensitive modules; skip rather than endanger

2 · The Backbone

Field Supervisor Logistics & Time Management

The Field Supervisor turns a list of households and a team of people into a smooth daily operation. The difference between a good day and a lost one is almost always made before 8 AM and at the midday checkpoint.

Start the team on time, plan the day before it begins

The morning launch, a supervisor's first job

The team that leaves late never recovers the morning interview block, and the morning is when farmers, daily-wage workers, and the elderly are reachable. Before the team departs, the supervisor: confirms each Surveyor's household assignments for the day; verifies devices are charged and the correct form version is loaded; distributes paper backups and referral cards; confirms transport; reviews any area-specific safety alerts; and clears up every question from yesterday's data so no one carries a doubt into the field.

Track progress household by household, not just a daily count

A supervisor does not wait until evening to learn that a Surveyor completed two interviews instead of six. Progress is tracked per household, through the day, against each Surveyor's plan, so a shortfall is caught while there is still time to redeploy, reassign a hard cluster, or send help.

The midday checkpoint, the most valuable 20 minutes of the day

The midday check-in, three questions for each Surveyor

At lunch, the supervisor reaches every Surveyor (in person or by call) and asks, in effect, three things: (1) How many are done, and are you on track for the plan? (2) What has gone wrong, or what is confusing?, a tricky respondent, an ambiguous question, a refusal pattern. (3) Is everyone safe and well?, heat, location, any incident. This is also the mandatory data-sync point. A shortfall or a confusion caught at 1 PM can still be fixed; the same problem discovered at 7 PM is a lost day.

Lunch & midday review

Lunch is a genuine break, rushed, skipped breaks lead to fatigued surveyors and careless data. It is also the natural moment for a quick midday review: confirm afternoon assignments and callbacks, redistribute work if someone is far behind, and resolve any standardisation question on the spot so the whole team records the same situation the same way.

The evening debrief, turn the day's data into tomorrow's improvement

Running a 30 to 45 minute daily feedback session

Productivity review (5 min), planned vs. completed per Surveyor; who is behind and why. Quality flags (10 min), today's short interviews, outliers, missing consent, skip violations, discussed with the responsible Surveyor as learning, not punishment. Field-problem sharing (10 min), open floor for "the respondent said X, how do I record it?"; every clarification here prevents fifty future errors. Inconsistency resolution (10 min), agree the correct interpretation and everyone writes it down. Tomorrow's plan (5 min), assign villages, distribute replacement households, confirm transport.

4 · Non-Negotiable

Safety & Security Protocols

Safety always takes priority over data collection. No interview is worth a safety risk.

Personal safety, the non-negotiable rules

Buddy system: never go to an unfamiliar or isolated area alone; minimum team size is two; female surveyors in remote areas are always paired. Location sharing: share live location with the supervisor at the start of every field day. Check-in schedule: contact the supervisor at midday and at the end of fieldwork; a missed check-in by 30+ minutes triggers supervisor contact, and an hour triggers the emergency protocol. Emergency card: carry supervisor, field manager, local police, nearest hospital/PHC, and the project helpline.

Gender-specific safety

Never travel alone to isolated areas; avoid field visits after dark, if an interview runs late, leave it for the next day. Report any harassment, from a respondent, community member, or teammate, immediately; if the supervisor is the issue, report directly to the field manager. The organisation maintains zero tolerance for harassment, handled confidentially, with no retaliation for reporting.

🗣️ Scenario, Heat-stroke warning signs (46°C, Barmer, Rajasthan)

At 12:30 PM a teammate is dizzy, his head feels heavy, and he has stopped sweating despite the heat, a critical danger sign. 🗣️ Scenario, Communal tension during fieldwork (western UP)

Sirens, shops closing, a shopkeeper says "there's been a clash, you should all leave." Stop all interviews immediately (do not "finish one more"); save in-progress forms as drafts; gather the team and verify the head-count; call the field manager ("local disturbance in [location], team withdrawing, all safe"); leave by the most direct safe route, avoiding crowds; do not engage, photograph, or take sides; remain at base until the field manager confirms it is safe. Affected interviews are logged "Suspended, community disturbance."

6 · Access

8 · The Memory System

Documentation, Field Notes, Incidents & Daily Logs

The CAPI form captures what the respondent said; documentation captures everything else, the context, atmosphere, and disruptions no questionnaire can anticipate. A well-documented operation can be reconstructed, audited, and defended; a poorly documented one cannot.

Field notes, written immediately after each interview, not at day's end: the respondent's demeanour and engagement; who else was present and their influence; anything important said outside the questionnaire; environmental cues that contextualise responses; disruptions; and the Surveyor's own uncertainties. Incident reports, filed within 24 hours of any deviation from normal operations (a factual record, not a complaint). Daily logs, every workday, capturing attempts vs. completions, refusals, callbacks, travel and fieldwork hours, devices used, sync status, and observations.

🗣️ Scenario, Documenting a hostile political encounter (South 24 Parganas, WB)

A party worker demands "party permission" for the survey and is verbally aggressive. The Surveyor stays calm, does not argue ("I'm from a research organisation, our work is separate from politics; let me call my supervisor"), steps away, saves the in-progress form, and calls the supervisor; if it can't be resolved remotely, he withdraws from the area for the day. That evening he files an incident report: date, time, location, what happened, his response, witnesses, the interviews affected, and a recommendation for supervisor/field-manager liaison with local leadership before re-entry.

2 · Independent Verification

Back-Checks, and How a Back-Checker Does a Great Job

A back-check is an independent re-visit to an already-interviewed household, usually within 3 to 7 days, to verify the human process behind the data: did the interview happen? Was it done properly? Was consent taken? Back-checks are run by the Field Monitor (independent of the team), on a random 10 to 15% of all interviews plus any the data team has flagged.

What a back-checker verifies

1. Confirmation of visit, "did someone from our organisation come to speak with you?" (asked neutrally, never "we’re checking if our enumerator came"). 2. Key-variable consistency, a few stable items (household size, number of children, dwelling type, land or bank ownership) compared with the original. 3. Consent process, was the study explained, permission asked, confidentiality stated? 4. Interview conduct, did they read from a device, seem rushed, skip questions?

How a back-checker does a great job

  • Stay independent and neutral. Never reveal which Surveyor visited or hint at the "right" answer. You are verifying, not accusing.
  • Build quick rapport first. A guarded respondent recalls less, a warm, brief introduction gets truer answers than a checklist tone.
  • Pick stable, memorable variables for comparison, things unlikely to change in a week and easy to recall, not opinions.
  • Treat recall failure fairly. In communities visited by many NGOs and officials, "I don’t remember who came" is common and is not proof of fabrication, probe gently before concluding.
  • Check GPS and timing against the original record as a quiet cross-reference.
  • Document every back-check formally, matched, minor, moderate, or major, regardless of outcome, so patterns are visible over time.
DiscrepancyExampleAction
MinorOriginal 5 household members, back-check 4Recall variation, no action; logged for monitoring

🗣️ Scenario, A respondent who doesn’t remember the interview (Gumla, Jharkhand)

"Many people come, I don’t remember who." This is not automatically fabrication. The back-checker probes gently ("did someone ask questions using a tablet last week?"), asks a few verification questions and compares them to the original, and checks whether the original GPS matches the dwelling. If key variables match, the interview likely occurred, classification Minor. If nothing matches and the respondent seems genuinely never interviewed, it escalates to Major. Either way, the resolution is documented.

3 · Seeing It Live

Spot-Checks & Accompaniments, the FS & FM Toolkit

Where a back-check looks backward at a finished interview, a spot-check looks at work as it happens. It is the single most powerful coaching tool a Field Supervisor or Field Monitor has.

What a spot-check is

A spot-check is an in-person check during live fieldwork. It can mean verifying that a Surveyor is actually at the assigned location and following protocol, or, most valuably, observing a real interview from start or part-way through. Spot-checks may be announced (planned, the Surveyor knows) or unannounced (a surprise visit), and every Surveyor should be spot-checked regularly, with more attention for new or struggling staff.

The three kinds of accompaniment

TypeWhat the observer doesBest for
Full accompanimentSits through an entire interview, from consent to close, observing silently and scoring against a checklistNew surveyors; a complete picture of technique, pacing, and protocol
Partial accompanimentObserves part of an interview, typically consent plus a module or two, then moves to another SurveyorCovering more staff in a day; checking a specific module or skill
Silent shadowObserves unobtrusively from a slight distance, barely participating, so the interview unfolds as naturally as possibleSeeing how a Surveyor really behaves when not being closely watched; rapport & respondent comfort

How to conduct a great spot-check, tips & technique

  • Be invisible during the interview. Do not interrupt, correct, prompt, or react, your presence already changes the room; minimise it. Sit slightly behind the Surveyor’s side, never between Surveyor and respondent.
  • Get consent for your presence. The Surveyor briefly introduces you, and the respondent agrees to a colleague observing for quality.
  • Observe, don’t teach, yet. Note everything; save all feedback for a private conversation after, never in front of the respondent.
  • Watch the whole craft: Was consent read fully? Questions read verbatim? Probing neutral? Privacy ensured before sensitive modules? GPS captured at the dwelling? Was the respondent treated with dignity and patience?
  • Coach with one or two priorities, not a list of twenty. Lead with something done well, then the most important fix.
  • Follow up. A spot-check that never leads to a second look is a missed opportunity, confirm the coaching landed.

  • Read the consent script fully; confirmed voluntary participation & confidentiality
  • Read every question as written, at the respondent’s pace, no leading or paraphrasing
  • Probed neutrally; offered full ranges; showed no surprise or judgment
  • Ensured privacy before sensitive modules; skipped rather than endangered
  • Handled refusals/"Don’t Know" correctly and recorded them honestly

🌼 A little field story

A Field Supervisor in Bihar quietly shadowed a nervous new Surveyor and noticed she apologised before every income question, which made respondents suspicious. A two-minute coaching tip afterwards ("ask it as routine, not as an intrusion") turned her into one of the team’s steadiest interviewers within a week. Spot-checks are not about catching people out; they are about catching people up.

Most quality problems have a recognisable signature in the data and a prevention a good Surveyor can apply in real time.

🗣️ Scenario, Ten respondents all earning exactly ₹10,000 (Gaya, Bihar)

Ten consecutive identical incomes, near-impossible in a real population. The Field Monitor investigates: a fixed brick-kiln wage for all is possible but unlikely; far more likely the Surveyor is accepting "about 10,000" without probing (satisficing) or entering a default (fabrication). An accompaniment or back-check resolves it, if the question was asked but not probed, coach on income precision ("is it the same every month? In a lean month? A good month?"); if not asked at all, escalate. The real-time lesson for the Surveyor: when you notice yourself recording the same value repeatedly, that is the signal to probe harder.

The Daily Quality Tracker & Survey Efficiency

Two people watch the numbers every day. The Project Associate reviews productivity and quality for each team and each person, daily. The Field Monitor tracks quality and survey efficiency across the 3 to 4 teams they oversee, spotting which teams and which field staff are thriving and which need help.

The Daily Quality Tracker, five dimensions, 20 points each

Each team is scored daily out of 100 across five dimensions: Consent compliance, Data completeness, Duration compliance, Probing quality (assessed via back-checks and accompaniments), and Tracking compliance (correct households, correct status codes, documented callbacks, visiting a wrong household is an automatic zero on that dimension). The Project Associate maintains the per-person and per-team scores; the Field Monitor reviews them across all their teams.

Tracking survey efficiency across teams & staff (Field Monitor)

Beyond quality, the Field Monitor watches efficiency: completions per Surveyor per day, callbacks cleared vs. pending, travel time vs. interview time, and how each team’s output compares to the others. The aim is not to rank people for its own sake, it is to see where help is needed: a team consistently behind may have a harder cluster, a transport problem, or one struggling member, and the Monitor redeploys support accordingly.

📊 The Quality Gate
The 90% rule: intensive daily feedback and mentoring continue for each team until its Daily Quality Score reaches 90 out of 100 for three consecutive days, confirmed by the research team. Only then can the feedback cadence ease for that team. Teams that never reach 90% are investigated, is a specific Surveyor dragging the score, or is the instrument still causing confusion?

Reading the numbers like a Field Monitor

One weak metric is a question; three weak metrics together are a diagnosis. A Surveyor simultaneously low on completion, short on duration, and high on missing data is almost certainly rushing and under-probing. The move is private, not public: a quiet word ("your interviews were a bit short last week and some answers are missing, is something feeling difficult?"), then an accompaniment to observe technique and coach live, and a follow-up the next week to confirm improvement.

6 · Putting It Right

Corrections & Re-Interviews

Errors happen; the system for fixing them protects the integrity of the data trail.

🛠️ The Correction Rule
Surveyors NEVER correct submitted data unilaterally. All corrections flow through the supervisor and are logged with the form ID, question, old value, new value, and justification.
SituationWho authorisesAudit trail
Obvious typo (age 220 → 22)Supervisor, from a data checkLogged with date, Surveyor ID, original & corrected values
Unit error (₹15000 where thousands expected → 15)Supervisor + data team, confirmed with the SurveyorLogged with the Surveyor’s confirmation noted
Respondent name/ID mismatchSupervisor, against the assignment sheetMismatch & resolution documented
Skip-logic errorData team + supervisorFull documentation; partial re-interview if the answer can’t be recovered

Re-interviews, when required

A re-interview is needed when data is lost (app crash, corruption) or when an investigation requires it. It is scheduled quickly (ideally within 48 hours to minimise recall decay), re-administers only the affected module, re-reading the prior section’s last few answers to re-orient, and is submitted flagged with the reason. Both datasets are preserved with clear provenance; re-interview data never silently replaces the original.

🗣️ Scenario, A crash wipes the agriculture module (Thanjavur, TN)

After a 70-minute interview the app crashes and Section 4 (~40 questions) loads blank; other modules are intact. The Surveyor reports it immediately; the supervisor checks for auto-save/cached recovery; if unrecoverable, a re-interview is scheduled within 48 hours. The Surveyor re-administers only Section 4 ("there was a problem with our device, I need to ask a few questions again"), starting by re-reading the last questions of Section 3, and submits it flagged "re-interview, Section 4, form corruption." The incident is logged with Surveyor ID, form ID, affected module, cause, and dates.

1 · Boundaries

Your Role, Clear Boundaries

A Surveyor has real responsibilities toward respondent welfare, and equally real limits. Knowing both is what makes a response helpful rather than harmful. The golden rule: notice, be kind, and report, then let the organisation’s trained people take it forward.

🗣️ Scenario, Caring vs. overstepping (Bundi, Rajasthan)

A respondent breaks down: her husband drinks, sold their only goat for liquor, and the children haven’t eaten since yesterday. The Surveyor wants to give money, buy food, confront the husband. Why not: giving money sets an expectation that surveys carry benefits (affecting every future interview); confronting the husband could endanger her after the Surveyor leaves; the Surveyor isn’t trained to intervene and could make things worse. What to do instead: listen with empathy ("what you’ve shared is very difficult, I’m listening"); complete or pause the interview as she wishes; and afterwards report to the supervisor and safeguarding lead, with the facts but no identifying details in the survey comments. The organisation’s safeguarding lead, not the Surveyor, decides how to help.

2 · Calibration

Recognising Distress, Notice, Be Kind, Report

The job is not to diagnose or treat, it is to notice, respond gently, and report according to protocol. Not every uncomfortable moment is a crisis, and not every crisis is visible.

LevelWhat it looks likeYour response
L1, Discomfort (normal)Shifting, pausing, a nervous laugh, "this is a strange question"Normal. "Some questions seem unusual, they’re part of our standard form." Continue with empathy
L2, Emotional distress (monitor)Voice cracks, eyes water, long silences, references to hardshipSlow down; offer a pause and water; let them decide whether to continue
L3, Acute distress (act)Crying openly, shaking, discloses abuse or violence, fear for safetyPause, stop asking questions; sit quietly and kindly; after leaving, report to the supervisor and safeguarding lead
L4, Immediate danger (emergency)Talk of self-harm; fresh injuries suggesting ongoing abuse; a child in immediate dangerSTOP. Stay calm and do not leave the person alone in acute distress. Call your supervisor at once, they and the safeguarding lead arrange the right professional support

What a Surveyor does, and does not do

Do: stay calm; listen without judgment; acknowledge the person’s feelings ("I hear you, and I understand this is very hard"); stop the survey if needed; and report promptly so trained people can help. Do not: dismiss ("don’t think like that"), moralise, investigate, ask a child to recount anything, promise specific outcomes, or try to counsel. Recognising and reporting is exactly the right amount, it is not your job to carry it alone.

Non-verbal signs worth noticing

Visible injuries (bruises, burn marks), do not ask "who did this?"; note it factually and report. Flinching at a spouse’s name or an opening door; whispering or watching the door; a child who appears malnourished, withdrawn, or fearful. You are not confirming anything, you are noticing and passing it to the safeguarding lead.

🗣️ Scenario, A respondent talks of having no reason to go on (Kalahandi, Odisha)

A widow with heavy debt says quietly that sometimes she feels there is no point in living. What the Surveyor does: stop the survey; stay calm and warm; acknowledge gently ("I heard what you said, and I understand this is a very difficult time"); do not dismiss it or lecture; do not leave her alone while she is in acute distress; and contact the supervisor immediately, who involves the safeguarding lead to arrange proper support. The Surveyor’s role is to respond with humanity and to bring in the people trained to help, not to counsel.

🗣️ Scenario, Observing without interrogating (Unnao, UP)

During a roster a child of about eight sits quietly with marks on her forearms and avoids eye contact. Do not ask the child "who did this?" or raise it with the respondent, either could re-traumatise the child or create danger. Complete the interview normally; note the child’s name and age from the roster; immediately after leaving, document the observation factually and call the supervisor; the safeguarding lead determines the next step under the Child Protection Policy. Discuss it with no one in the village or team except the supervisor.

4 · Integrity in Action

Speaking Up & Reporting

Day to day, the most important safeguarding act is simple: if you see something wrong during work, you report it. Speaking up in good faith is always protected, and it is what keeps the whole study trustworthy.

What to speak up about

  • Any unethical act by a colleague or anyone during work, fabricating or falsifying data, breaching a respondent’s confidentiality, mistreating or disrespecting a respondent.
  • Bribery, favours, or accepting/offering gifts in exchange for anything.
  • Harassment or discrimination of any kind, against staff or respondents (report under POSH).
  • Any concern about a child’s safety (report under the CPP, mandatory and immediate).
  • Safety violations, e.g. riding without a helmet, unsafe transport, ignoring withdrawal protocols during unrest.
  • Pressure on you to cut corners, skip consent, or hide a problem.

How to report, and what is guaranteed

Report to your Field Supervisor or Field Monitor, or use the organisation’s designated reporting channel (including the confidential / anonymous route for integrity concerns). Reports are handled in line with the relevant policy. You will never face retaliation for reporting in good faith, if you ever feel pressured not to report, escalate that pressure to the next level.

✅ The Threshold for Speaking Up
When in doubt, speak up. It is always better to raise something that turns out to be nothing than to stay silent about something that turns out to matter. The words are simple: "I saw something during work today that I think I should report, I’d like someone more senior to look into it." That is all it takes; the system does the rest.
🌼 A little field story

A first-week Surveyor noticed a teammate filling a form while sitting at a tea stall, not at a household. It felt awkward to mention, but she quietly told her Field Supervisor. The interview was re-done properly, the teammate was coached, and no one was punished for the honest report. Speaking up early kept one mistake from becoming the team’s problem.

6 · Sustaining the Work

Self-Care on Sensitive Topics

Hearing difficult things, week after week, takes a real toll. Recognising and managing it is a team responsibility, not a personal weakness.

🗣️ Scenario, Vicarious trauma (Beed, Maharashtra)

After three weeks on a sensitive module, a Surveyor can’t stop thinking about one respondent, snaps at teammates, dreads the daily departure, and has cried alone some evenings. The path to support: at a debrief the supervisor gently asks if the week was especially difficult; she first says "I’m fine" (a common response); the supervisor doesn’t push, but privately names it without judgment ("three weeks on this module is a lot, many people find it difficult; it is not weakness, would you like to switch to a different module this week?"); she accepts the rotation, is connected to the organisation’s support, and returns with renewed capacity. Rotating staff off heavy modules is a deliberate management strategy, not a favour.

What supervisors and monitors do for the team

Watch for changes in mood and engagement; create private, judgment-free openings to talk; rotate staff off heavy modules before burnout; connect people to the organisation’s support; and protect anyone who raises a concern from any form of retaliation. Sustaining the people is part of sustaining the data.
